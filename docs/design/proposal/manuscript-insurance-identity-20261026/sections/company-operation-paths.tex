\section{Repeated operations and consecutive company years}
\label{sec:company-operation-paths}

An unpaid customer claim does not disappear when the annual accounts close.
Nor does a newly purchased asset lose its service date. A simulator that starts
each year from account totals alone can balance the next set of statements
while collecting the wrong invoice or charging the wrong depreciation. The
company-path extension therefore carries the claims and asset cohorts alongside
cash, inventory, debt and equity. It also replaces the single annual purchase
and delivery with individually identified operations.

Each purchase supplies units and a new payable identity. Each delivery supplies
units, price, the control-transfer assumption and a new receivable identity.
Collections and supplier payments allocate explicit amounts to existing claims.
Payroll, plant additions and dividends may repeat. The selected annual methods
still require one depreciation calculation, legacy coupon, tax calculation and
close, together with the one specified bridge loan. Repeating ordinary business
operations does not repeat the tax or closing entry.

The distinction between recognition and settlement is preserved at each step.
A customer payment reduces its receivable; it does not recognise revenue again.
A supplier payment reduces its payable; it does not acquire inventory again.
An operation checks every allocation, its journal and the resulting cash or
stock before applying any change. If the second allocation overpays a claim,
the first allocation in that same operation is also refused. Earlier completed
operations remain in the conditional history. Reusing a settled claim's identity
is invalid even when its outstanding balance is zero.

\paragraph{When splitting operations preserves the accounts.}
Under the example's homogeneous cost, a purchase of 34 units at 5 can be
represented as two identified purchases of 17 units each. The payable total
remains 170, but there are now two claims against which payments must be
allocated. Corresponding splits of sales, receipts, supplier payments, payroll,
capex and dividends reproduce all annual statements when their dates, dependency
order, unit costs and asset lives remain unchanged. The checks compare every
original journal for 18 rate/share/borrowing-cost paths and the aggregate
journals of four proportional split examples. Balance alone is not the test.

Timing can nevertheless change feasibility. If half the baseline receipts,
140, arrives before bridge repayment and payroll, the company can pay principal
85.4, the small bridge interest charge and payroll 50. The remaining cash is
positive. If only 40 percent, or 112, arrives, it can repay the bridge and pay
the first payroll instalment of 20, but the remaining payroll of 30 fails by
23.4 plus bridge interest. The prior repayment, collection and payroll remain;
the failed payment has no accounting effect. At a shared instant, the explicit
operation order determines whether a collection precedes a payment. Future
cash is not available merely because its timestamp matches the payment's.

\paragraph{The next year starts from economic memory.}
Northstar carries eight inventory units into 2026. The next year's authored
demand of 32 and target stock of 10 require another 34 purchased units, costing
170. Selling 32 units at 11 produces revenue 352 and cost of sales 160. The
company collects its 55 of opening receivables and 90 percent of the new sales,
pays its 40 of opening payables and 160 of current purchases, and pays payroll
55. These are explicit synthetic choices, including collection before the
supplier payment; they are not estimated operating behaviour.

The asset acquired for 20 in 2025 enters service on 1 January 2026 and contributes
2 of depreciation. The earlier cohorts still contribute 10, 2 and 1, so the
annual total is 15. With legacy interest 6, pre-tax profit is
\(352-160-55-15-6=116\). The retained immediate 20 percent tax assumption
gives profit 92.8. Operating cash is 87.6; after capex and dividends of 10 each,
cash increases by 67.6. Every one of the 29 report lines is checked against
independent arithmetic. A third authored year carries the same claim and
cohort memory again. The joined case executes 63 operations across three years
and retains 14 distinct claim identities, including those already settled.

\paragraph{A revision must say what changes.}
The annual authoring rule in Section~\ref{sec:company-input-authoring} collects
90 percent of new sales. Its price change from 10 to 11 therefore increases
collections by 27, tax by 6 and cash by 21. A list of already authored settlement
amounts has a different meaning. If only the delivery price changes, receipts
stay fixed: profit rises by 24, receivables by 30 and cash falls by 6 through
the additional tax. Neither result is a balancing convention. They answer
different instructions about whether the collection policy is regenerated.

The same distinction persists across years. Increasing 2026's price from 11
to 12 while retaining its explicit collection amounts raises profit by 25.6,
reduces cash by 6.4 and leaves 32 of additional receivables. Those claims remain
in 2027 unless a later settlement is changed as well. The 2025 result is
unchanged. Revising 2025 instead recomputes all affected later years from the
original historical opening. Each conditional result identifies its input and
predecessor; an old suffix cannot be joined to the revised opening. This is a
new branch of assumptions, not a restatement of an observed historical book.

An unknown payroll amount at a known instant stops at that operation and retains
the preceding completed operations. An unknown event instant prevents a reliable
dated schedule. In either case an incomplete 2026 result cannot supply a 2027
opening. Reports keep the earlier complete years and any valid dated prefix,
while uncomputed annual cash and profit remain unresolved.

\paragraph{A coherent path still needs the right information.}
The historical opening has its own source dependencies. The new evidence packet
validates the complete reachable source and admission records, rather than just
the opening balances. It also binds each of the fourteen operation methods to
its payload definition, written rule, checked implementation and conformance
evidence. Conditional closing records preserve full economic memory and identify
their preceding result; they are not inserted into the historical Facts records.
The packet contains 42 records and 35 exact byte payloads. These identities
support reproducible local checking, not an external accounting approval or a
signature establishing the truth of the inputs.

Source timing is consequential even in the synthetic example. Seven required
source units carry a publication date of 2 March 2025, after the start of the
first simulated year. They are ineligible under a 1 January cutoff. A cutoff
during 2 March remains unresolved because the publication time is unknown;
the next day passes that source-date test. System-as-of checks also distinguish
ingestion from acceptance. At 12:30 UTC on 5 March, the sources have been
ingested but two required acceptances at 13:00 UTC have not occurred.

Passing a later source cutoff does not establish when the future operating
assumptions were authored or available to a decision-maker. The checked export
therefore supports archival synthetic simulation. Investor-information or
system-as-of use of the complete path remains unresolved until its assumptions
and decision information are admitted as well. This preserves the distinction
between a conditional accounting consequence and a forecast that could actually
have been made at the stated date.
