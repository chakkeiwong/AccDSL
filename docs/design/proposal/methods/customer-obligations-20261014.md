## W1 — Separately purchasable warranty service

### Inputs, classification and state

The contract combines a product with a separately purchasable service warranty.
Under B29, the latter is a distinct service; B28–B33 distinguish assurance
from additional service. The two promises satisfy paragraphs 27–30. At
inception, comparable standalone selling prices are 100 and 20; the fixed
bundle price is 108. Neither the discount-allocation exception in 81–83 nor
the variable-consideration exception in 84–86 applies. A list/invoice price
alone would not establish either standalone price (77).

The service is a uniform stand-ready promise from 1 July 2024 to 30 June
2025. The customer receives and consumes its benefits as supplied under
35(a); the admitted progress measure faithfully represents this uniform
service under 39–45/B14–B19. The admitted monthly measure assigns each
completed service month one twelfth of the allocation; six completed months
therefore represent half the performance. This is not a daily day-count
fraction inferred from the dates. Dates alone cannot establish that for
another promise. The product is delivered with control on 1 July 2024.
Opening inventory/equity is 60. Cash costs of service are 3 per half-year;
they are recognized as supplied and paid, without a separate eligible cost
asset. The example contains no refund or material financing component.

State retains the original 108 price, two standalone prices and their
evidence, allocations 90 and 18, product delivery identity, cumulative
service progress/revenue, cash receipts, service costs and contract liability.
No change in later standalone prices silently changes the inception allocation.

### Procedure and reports

1. Compute product allocation `108×100/(100+20)=90` and service allocation
   `108×20/(100+20)=18` under 73–86. Require their sum to equal 108.
2. Receipt before performance: debit cash 108, credit contract liability 108.
3. Product control transfer: debit contract liability 90, credit revenue 90;
   debit cost of sales 60, credit inventory 60. The 18 service obligation
   survives delivery. Repeating the delivery cannot recognize it again.
4. At 31 December 2024, supported cumulative progress is 1/2. Target service
   revenue is 9; post only the difference from prior recognized service
   revenue: debit liability 9, credit revenue 9. Debit service expense 3,
   credit cash 3 for actual service cost.
5. On 30 June 2025, progress is 1. Release the remaining 9 and recognize/pay
   remaining service costs 3. The contract liability finishes at zero.

Year-one revenue 99, expense 63, profit 36; cash 105, liability 9, equity 96.
Year-two revenue 9, expense 3, profit 6; cash/equity 102. Cumulative revenue
108, expense 66 and profit 42. Direct operating cash is 105 then −3; the
indirect bridges are `36+60+9=105` and `6−9=−3`. All other cash categories
are zero in this specified slice. Period revenue is not cumulative revenue
posted a second time.

### Adverse and neighboring cases

Recognizing all 108 at product delivery is wrong for these promises: 18
remains unperformed. Invoice labels, months elapsed without eligible faithful
progress, or unknown standalone prices cannot authorize that release. If
progress cannot reasonably be measured, 44–45 requires an assessment of the
separate cost-recovery conditions; this percentage method returns unresolved.

Without a separate purchase option, B30 sends assurance-only warranties to
AASB 137 unless there is an additional service. Law, duration and tasks are
indicators (B31), not automatic numeric cutoffs. B32 makes inseparable
assurance/service warranties one performance obligation when they cannot
reasonably be accounted for separately. B33 harm or infringement compensation
is not a service performance obligation. Exact Australian AASB 137 measurement
authority is not inspected here: return `requires_assurance_method`, retaining
the obligation and missing inputs. The historical EU IAS 37 examples do not
silently admit it. This claim limit leaves the supported service case usable.

## M1 — Modification of remaining distinct goods

### Facts and classification

Original contract: 10 distinct units for 100, prepaid; four transferred before
the modification, earning 40. An approved enforceable change adds six units
for 48, prepaid at amendment. Their comparable standalone price remains 10
per unit, with no appropriate contract-specific adjustment making 48 reflect
standalone selling prices. The remaining goods are distinct from those already
transferred. Paragraph 20's separate-contract test therefore fails and 21(a)
applies. The conclusion uses these findings, not the number 48 alone.

For a complete cost and statement slice, opening inventory is 16 homogeneous
units at 6 each, financed by equity 96. This includes six initially uncommitted
units. The contract subledger retains the original price and deliveries,
modification identity/date/approval/enforceability, classification facts,
remaining consideration/quantities, allocation version and later deliveries.
Stock state retains actual unit allocations. No inventory is created by the
contract modification.

### Procedure and reports

The first receipt posts cash/liability 100. Four deliveries release revenue
40 and cost 24. Immediately before amendment cash 100 plus inventory 72
equals liability 60 plus equity 112. Freeze that history. At amendment,
combine unrecognized original consideration 60 with new consideration 48.
Allocate the 108 over 12 remaining equal standalone-price units: 9 each.
The receipt adds cash/liability 48. The modification changes remaining
performance state; it earns no new revenue merely on approval or cash receipt.

Deliver six more units by year end: debit liability 54/credit revenue 54;
debit cost of sales 36/credit inventory 36. Year-one revenue 94, cost 60,
profit 34, cash 148, inventory 36, liability 54 and equity 130. Direct
operating cash 148 equals `34+60+54`. In the next period deliver the remaining
six: revenue 54, cost 36 and profit 18; cash/equity 148, no inventory or
contract liability. Operating cash is zero, independently `18+36−54=0`.
Lifetime revenue 148 and cost 96 give profit 52.

For comparison, addition of six units for 60 **with** the paragraph 20
standalone-price finding is a separate contract. The original remaining
six earn 60 and the new six earn 60; the first 40 stays intact. Both
contracts retain their own identifiers/allocations, even though this matched
example permits a total of 160. Separate-contract classification is not
inferred merely from comparing nominal prices.

The tempting reallocation `148/16` gives 9.25 per unit and reduces the first
four units from 40 to 37. That is wrong for the stated 21(a) facts: only
remaining performance is reallocated. An unapproved proposal leaves the
existing contract operative (18). Unknown enforceability remains unresolved.
Approved scope with undetermined price invokes 19 and its variable-consideration
and constraint assessment; the fixed-price example must not use price zero.

## M2 — Modification of an integrated performance obligation

The customer controls work in progress as created under 35(b). The existing
single integrated obligation is partially satisfied, and the remaining work
is not distinct from work already transferred. Original fixed price 100 and
supported output progress 40% yielded cumulative revenue 40. An approved
enforceable change expands the same integrated promise to price 130; a
revised faithful output assessment is 30% of the modified promise. These
progress inputs are supported assumptions, not ratios deduced from cash costs.

Under 21(b), revised cumulative earned revenue is `130×0.30=39`; subtract
the 40 already recognized to obtain a catch-up of −1. Preserve both the
previous information view and the amended current view. This is a modification
effect, not evidence that the original estimate was an error. The operation
receives the revised promise/price/progress and prior cumulative revenue;
it proposes only the difference, linked to the approved modification.

For the prepaid slice, opening cash/equity is 50. Receive 100 and incur/pay
service costs 30 before amendment. Recognition of 40 gives cash 120,
liability 60, cumulative profit 10 and equity 60. Receive the additional 30
and post the catch-up: debit revenue 1, credit liability 1. The current
balances are cash 150, liability 91, equity 59; cumulative revenue 39,
expense 30 and profit 9. Operating cash since opening is 100, also
`9+91=100`. The amendment receipt by itself does not earn 30.

Completion in the next period releases liability/revenue 91; further
service expenses/cash are 60. Period profit is 31, operating cash −60,
also `31−91=−60`. Closing cash/equity is 90. Lifetime revenue 130 less
cost 90 gives profit 40 on opening equity 50. Different collection schedules
use the established CA/CL/AR method; this prepaid journal cannot be reused
unchanged if the balance requiring adjustment is a contract asset.

Paragraph 21(c) covers mixed remaining obligations. Return a required explicit
allocation between components consistent with 21; do not force the entire
change into a binary distinct/integrated choice. This unit does not compute a
mixed case. A transaction-price update without changed enforceable scope/price
rights is assessed under 87–89. Subsequent changes after a modification need
90: pre-modification variable consideration can be allocated to earlier
obligations under 90(a), while 90(b) governs other non-separate modifications.
The fixed-price example establishes no automatic rule for those updates.

## A1 — Principal and agent with matched cash

Identify each specified good or service, then assess control before transfer
(B34–B38, 33). An entity may be principal for one promise and agent for another.
Primary responsibility, inventory risk and price discretion inform the
control finding; none is a mechanical substitute. Momentary legal title does
not settle control. Record the relevant rights/obligations and finding, not a
user-selectable gross/net accounting preference. Unknown control yields an
unresolved classification, preserving the competing conditional consequences.

In the principal case, the seller controls an identified good before transfer,
can direct its use and obtains its benefits, is responsible for its delivery,
and owes the supplier 100. It acquires the good on credit (debit inventory
100/credit payable 100), then transfers it to the customer and receives 120
(debit cash 120/credit revenue 120; debit cost of sales 100/credit inventory
100). Payment of the supplier debits payable 100/credits cash 100. From zero
opening balances, final cash/equity and profit are 20.

In the agent case, the supplier controls the good until customer transfer;
the entity only arranges that transfer, completes the arranging service and
is entitled to a fixed commission 20. It receives cash 120 under a collection
arrangement and owes the supplier 100. The cash is controlled by the agent,
with a recognized remittance obligation; fiduciary assets outside its balance
sheet are excluded from this example. Post debit cash 120, credit payable
100 and credit commission revenue 20; then settle payable/cash 100. There is
no agent inventory or supplier cost of sales. Final cash/equity and profit
are again 20, but revenue is 20 instead of 120.

Between receipt and remittance both cases show cash 120, payable 100 and
equity 20. Matching cash and profit do not make the underlying control facts
identical. Retain the receipt and remittance legs in the cash ledger. Whether
the cash-flow statement may present those flows net requires its own AASB
107 conditions; revenue classification alone does not decide that presentation.
The documentary result reports the two cash legs and their net change without
claiming an agency gross/net cash-flow election. Both cases have no OCI,
owner movement, remaining receivable, inventory or liability at completion;
tax remains uncomputed.
