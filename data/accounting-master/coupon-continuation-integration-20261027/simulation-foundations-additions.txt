to subsequent accounting and recovery events. Refusing a payment instruction must therefore
preserve the outstanding obligation and the failed history rather than remove it from later
statements.

17.29.1
Closing the period preserves the debt and the missed decision
The stopped state is dated 31 December 2027, the last day of the declared period. A selected
closing operation transfers the temporary income and expense balances into equity and opens
the next period on 1 January 2028. Under the signed-book convention, the loss produces a
debit capital balance 89/16, representing negative equity −89/16. Cash remains 85/16, the
signed interest-payable balance remains −43/8, and the second coupon’s amount paid remains
zero. The facility balance stays zero and its used flag stays true.
The opening period has no cash flows yet. Earlier flows remain in the closed book, and an
associated archive retains the original refusal, the unexecuted commands and the identity of
the new opening. Carrying balances alone would lose the distinction between a missed payment
and an ordinary unpaid accrual. Conversely, automatically resetting the used flag would invent
a fresh borrowing right. The generic typed carry engine can express such a reset, but this
selected coupon-preservation rule rejects it. States stopped on 30 June are left unassessed by
this year-end operation; they are not silently advanced to December.26
17.29.2
A new instruction can reduce the claim without rewriting history
Suppose an explicitly supplied instruction pays 85/16 on 2 January 2028. This amount uses
the carried cash; it is not supplied by a new loan or inferred managerial policy. The selected
late-payment method reduces cash and the unpaid coupon by the same amount, and adds
that amount to cumulative payment toward the second coupon. Let C be current cash, U the
positive unpaid coupon and P its cumulative amount paid. For an instructed partial payment
q, the accounting and memory changes are
C′ = C −q,
U′ = U −q,
P ′ = P + q,
0 < q < U,
q ≤C.
(17.44)
Here U = I2 −P, with the originally accrued coupon I2 unchanged. Thus U′ +P ′ = I2: payment
discharges part of the existing claim and creates no second expense. The strict inequality q < U
gives this selected method a genuinely partial settlement domain. Full settlement would require
a distinct supported instruction and sufficient cash.
For the supplied instruction, C′ = 0, U′ = 1/16 and P ′ = 85/16. The earlier action remains
none, because the original second payment was refused. A new partial2 phase records the later
settlement, while the used right and original refusal remain intact. Reusing the old policy solver
with this new phase would require a separate compatibility argument; cumulative payment does
not turn the original failed policy into a successful one.
Two supplied payments of 85/32 on 2 and 3 January produce the same final balances and
cumulative payment. They produce two dated cash flows and a different last-event date, so
equality of financial balances is not equality of histories. The report classifies these payments
as operating under the inherited synthetic convention. Complete comparisons on both account
charts retain the distinction between the two instruction schedules.27
