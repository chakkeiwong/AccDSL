17.7
Integer quantities and minimum lots
Shares, bond lots and physical units may be discrete. If a company needs net cash 10 and
shares sell for 3 with no fees, a continuous calculation requires 10/3 shares. If only whole shares
can be issued, four shares raise 12. The excess cash and dilution are real consequences of the
lot constraint.
A minimum issuance size can make a small funding need expensive or infeasible. A fixed fee
can make splitting one issue into two change net proceeds. These effects break the proportional
invariance that holds for a simple linear loan.
The model should specify whether quantities are continuous approximations or exact units. For
large public share counts, a continuous approximation may be immaterial for some queries. For
a closely held company or indivisible asset, it may dominate the decision. The approximation
needs a bound tied to the question.
17.8
Branch-and-bound interpretation
A mixed discrete-continuous family can be explored through bounds on regions. If a region’s
best possible result cannot improve the incumbent for an optimization query, it can be pruned
under the method’s correctness conditions. If a region is infeasible, it can be removed with
evidence.
Pruning for computational convenience is different. A low-probability region cannot be removed
from a worst-case query merely because it is unlikely. A region far from current data may still
be admissible if the observation model permits it. The pruning reason belongs in the result
record.
For a safety query, an outer lower cash bound above the requirement can close a region as safe.
A feasible witness below it establishes possible failure. Regions whose bounds straddle the
threshold remain unresolved and receive refinement priority. This approach can concentrate
work where it changes the answer.
The procedure may still face exponential growth. Resource exhaustion should preserve the
incumbent witnesses, certified bounds and unresolved regions. Reporting only the best found
point hides the remaining uncertainty and can reverse the meaning of a worst-case result.
17.9
Sensitivity of identified sets
A small source revision can change a feasible family discontinuously when constraints meet at a
boundary. Suppose exact observations require x = 1 and an interpretation requires x ≤1. The
family is nonempty. Revising the observation to x = 1.001 makes it empty if no tolerance or
other interpretation is admitted.
This does not mean the company changed abruptly. It may reveal that exact equality was too
strong for a rounded report. Observation precision and numerical tolerance should be audited
before interpreting a discontinuity economically.
Conversely, a small genuine change can cross a contractual threshold and have a large effect.
Smoothing it away would hide a real mechanism. The analyst must distinguish measurement
uncertainty from contractual discontinuity. Both can occur near the same number but require
different treatment.
A useful sensitivity report varies source precision, interpretation choices and economic shocks

17.10
A bounded implementation profile
An initial exact profile can support rational linear constraints, finite interpretation choices
and piecewise affine event rules. It can return feasible witnesses, exact or certified bounds,
and guarded outcome formulas. Nonlinear valuation and unsupported clauses remain explicit
extensions.
The profile should reject incompatible units, unbound variables and missing event premises before
solving. It should preserve unknown amounts as variables with justified domains. Arbitrary
large bounds inserted to make a solver finite should be labeled as computational assumptions
and tested for sensitivity.
A later nonlinear profile can use interval methods, constrained optimization or sampling, but
must retain the original query target and distinguish local from global results. A local minimum
of cash is not a global worst case. A successful solver exit code is not a proof of completeness.
The practical advantage of a bounded profile is that its claims can be checked. It does not
redefine the whole mission to exclude difficult contracts. Instead, it provides a reliable core and
a precise account of which additional methods are needed for broader company simulation.

17.11.3
Information can prevent an otherwise affordable action
For the moment retain only histories with a committed facility. Every detailed history admits
one of the two orders. This does not imply that the manager can select a feasible order using
the information available at the decision date.
Under the coarse signal, high means only that r > 0.04. It includes floating rates with interest
below 5.5, where payment is possible but drawing is forbidden, and rates above 0.055, where
drawing is possible but ordinary payment fails. The manager must issue the same order at
indistinguishable histories. Neither order works throughout this signal class.
Now suppose a second, explicit pre-decision signal reveals whether interest exceeds 5.5. The
manager can pay when the trigger is false and draw and pay when it is true. This rule is feasible
throughout the committed-facility family. Its feasibility comes from the additional information,
not from a better numerical approximation. If unavailable facilities are restored to the family,
some high-rate floating histories admit neither order. Even complete information cannot create
borrowing capacity.

histories with observation o, and let A(h) be the actions feasible at history h. A deterministic

17.11.4
What remains safe when calculation is unfinished
A computation budget can stop evaluation of individual regions without removing them from
the family. In the reference, the partition is constructed first and transitions are evaluated when
requested. If only one region has been evaluated, the others remain explicitly unresolved. The
coupon still has the valid outer bound 3 ≤I ≤6, but that bound alone does not identify a
feasible payment policy. Eager construction of the partition itself can become expensive when
more continuous variables or contractual alternatives are added.

Reference computations that
distinguish a useful approximation
from a wrong answer
A method can be tested most clearly when a small version of its target is known exactly. This
chapter constructs such targets for cash bounds, breach probabilities, policy comparison and
residual minimization. The calculations are analytic reference cases. They are not a benchmark
ranking and do not supply empirical evidence about a real company. Their role is to expose
errors that a large simulation can conceal behind apparently stable averages.
18.1
An exact distribution from a declared prior
Return to cash C = 90−2α, and now deliberately assume α is uniform on [0, 1]. This assumption
is not inferred from the financial statements. It creates a transparent probabilistic reference.
Cash is uniform on [88, 90], with mean 89 and variance 4/12 = 1/3.
For a covenant requiring cash at least 89.4, breach occurs when α > 0.3. Its probability is 0.7.
The expected cash conditional on breach is the mean of the interval [88, 89.4), namely 88.7.
These quantities answer different questions: unconditional mean, breach frequency and severity
conditional on breach.
A simulation with the right mean but the wrong breach probability is not accurate for covenant
analysis. For example, a two-point approximation placing equal mass at 88 and 90 has mean 89
but breach probability one half. Matching the first moment does not preserve threshold events.
The exact distribution also gives quantiles. Under the usual left-continuous generalized-inverse
convention, the p-quantile is 88 + 2p for 0 < p < 1, with endpoint values defined by continuous
extension. The convention should be explicit when discrete mass points are introduced, because
quantile definitions can differ at jumps.
18.2
A discrete grid with known bias
Approximate α by equally weighted midpoints (i −1/2)/n, for i = 1, . . . , n. The mean of the
grid remains one half, so mean cash is exactly 89 for every n. Breach frequency is the fraction
of midpoints above 0.3. For n = 4, midpoints are 0.125, 0.375, 0.625 and 0.875, giving breach
probability 0.75.
