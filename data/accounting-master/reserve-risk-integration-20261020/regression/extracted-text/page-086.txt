Input completeness and output meaning must also be explicit. A model that fixes the available
event templates and contract slots imposes a scope restriction; it does not establish that a
public filing lists every economic event. Nor does that restriction entitle a display to interpret
every omitted row as false.
Libkin’s primary proof compares information by reverse inclusion of possible completions: an
object is more informative when fewer complete objects remain compatible with it. For a query
to be evaluated directly on an incomplete object, the chosen output interpretation must preserve
that order as input information increases. The query must also respect the relevant renamings,
and its output domain must have a representation system whose formulas describe each object’s
complete interpretations (Libkin, 2014, Sections 6.1–6.2, equations (9)–(10)). In Theorem 2,
the proof first shows that evaluating the query preserves exactly the statements true in all
completed answers; it then uses those statements to establish the greatest-lower-bound answer
as an object (Libkin, 2016a, Definitions 4–7 and Theorem 2, pp. 11–18). Monotonicity alone is
insufficient, and SQL null comparisons need not meet the genericity condition (Libkin, 2016a,
Section 7.1, pp. 18–19).
The relational examples make the difference between input and output assumptions concrete.
Positive existential queries have the stated result with open-world inputs and answers. A
guarded positive fragment also has it with closed-world inputs and open-world answers; the
latter is an interpretation of omitted result rows, not a claim that the input filing lists every
economic event (Libkin, 2016a, Proposition 9 and Corollary 3, p. 20). These results do not
authorize evaluating arbitrary financial negation, aggregates or nonlinear constraints by treating
unknown amounts as ordinary constants. AccDSL has not established the theorem’s abstract
domain, information order and representation-system conditions for every financial output. The
constructed payable example above instead follows directly from its two declared histories.
A related relational method illustrates what a sound but incomplete answer means in practice.
For marked-null tables under closed-world set semantics, Libkin’s modified three-valued evaluator
returns a certainly positive answer when it yields 1 and a certainly negative answer when it
yields 0; its value 1/2 carries no guarantee (Libkin, 2016b, Theorems 4.1 and 5.3, pp. 8–12).
Let T = {(1, ⊥)} and ask whether there is a y for which T(1, y) and either y = 1 or y ̸= 1.
Every completed table answers yes, yet the modified evaluation leaves the answer at 1/2: its
null comparison and its negation are both unknown. The paper gives this case as a missed
certain answer for ordinary SQL; the modified evaluator’s outcome follows from its atomic rules.
An inconclusive evaluation therefore does not show that two possible histories give opposite
answers. AccDSL requires witnesses on both sides to establish that stronger claim; a timeout or
unsupported operation remains undecided. The relational guarantee itself does not transfer to
financial totals, nonlinear valuations, temporal histories or scenario conditioning.
The query contract records whether it returns a proposition, individually certain rows, or a
family of answers, together with the family scope and completeness and the interpretation
of omitted rows. Unsupported evaluation and unfinished search keep their existing failure or
unresolved status. In particular, a sampled answer family cannot be relabelled as the full family
merely because its displayed rows agree.
6.3
Why published statements cannot identify all transactions
The simplest derivation uses fixed account mappings and a fixed basis. Let z ∈Rn contain
unknown historical movement amounts. For m disclosed aggregates, let A ∈Rm×n be the
aggregation matrix and y = Az ∈Rm. If there is a nonzero vector v with Av = 0, then
A(z + v) = Az + Av = y.
