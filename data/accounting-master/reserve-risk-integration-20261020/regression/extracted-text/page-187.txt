On 1 July the parent receives 100 for a new instrument that matures on 1 October. Its original
terms require a coupon of 4, followed by either principal repayment of 92 or conversion of that
principal into six ordinary shares. The fixed conversion feature qualifies as equity in the parent’s
functional currency; no fees, participation or other consequential earnings effects are assumed.

Issue debits cash 100 and credits liability 92 and conversion equity 8. Effective interest of
4 raises the liability to 96. Paying the coupon returns it to 92. At maturity, original-terms
conversion transfers that 92 to ordinary equity and issues six shares without cash; redemption
pays 92 instead. The original equity 8 remains equity in either world (European Commission,
2023a, IAS 32, paragraphs 28–32 and AG32). This October-maturity contract differs from the
preceding January-maturity early-conversion illustration.
Tax requires its own facts. Assume a synthetic fiscal allocation of 92 to the debt and 8 to
equity, effective-interest deductibility at accrual, a 25% rate, and no additional tax on coupon
payment, redemption or conversion. The liability’s tax base then follows its carrying amount.
No new temporary difference arises under these supplied premises. A rate alone would not
justify that conclusion: IAS 12.23 describes jurisdictions whose initial tax base equals the
combined proceeds, producing a deferred tax liability charged to equity. Missing or different
tax-base findings prevent this particular company extension; they require the corresponding tax
method (European Commission, 2023a, IAS 12, paragraphs 8, 23, 58 and 61A).
The parent’s annual taxable income falls from 42 to 38. Current tax falls by 1; after the 8
already paid in the first half, its second-half payment is 1.5. The subsidiary’s tax and both
companies’ existing deferred-tax consequences are unchanged. Group pretax profit falls from 136
to 132, current tax from 28 to 27, and deferred-tax expense remains 6. Group profit is therefore
99. Non-controlling interests still receive 7.35 because the new interest belongs to the parent.
Ordinary earnings are 99 −7.35 = 91.65, not group profit 99 or the parent’s separate-company
profit 46.5.
The two settlements have identical earnings but different cash and ownership consequences. With
conversion, group cash becomes 644+100−4+1 = 741 and equity becomes 724+100−3 = 821:
issue and conversion add 100 of equity, while after-tax interest reduces earnings by 3. With
redemption, cash becomes 644+100−4−92+1 = 649 and equity becomes 724+8−3 = 729. In
both worlds, external operating receipts remain 250 and payments become 99; financing receipts
are 100. Financing payments remain 110 with conversion and rise to 202 with redemption. Net
cash movements of 141 and 49 reconcile to opening cash 600. Noncash conversion is excluded
from those cash flows.
