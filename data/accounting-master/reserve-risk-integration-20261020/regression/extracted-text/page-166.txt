9.11
When a component reaches its next accounting event
A recognised balance also promises a subsequent method. A refund liability must eventually be
settled; an impaired asset may recover; a modified lease must continue through payments and
depreciation. The following methods specify those transitions under stated facts. They use the
retained historical IFRS text, with current authority and actual-company measurement kept as
separate admission questions (European Commission, 2023a).
9.11.1
Actual returns can differ from the estimate
Section 2.7 follows two expected and actual returns. More generally, let L be the opening refund
liability, A the recovery asset, P the actual refund payment, K the actual selling-handling cost
and V the returned inventory admitted after deterioration and its NRV ceiling. When all rights
have closed, the debit-positive entries are
∆cash = −P −K,
∆inventory = V,
(9.3)
∆refundliability = L,
∆recoveryasset = −A,
(9.4)
∆revenue = P −L,
∆costofsales = A −V,
(9.5)
∆sellingexpense = K.
(9.6)
The debit-positive revenue amount is negative when releasing an excessive refund estimate
increases revenue. These entries sum to zero. The separation of selling cost follows the explicit
handling classification and IAS 2.16; IFRS 15.B25 governs the recovery asset. If handling
instead creates eligible inventory cost, that needs its own admitted classification.
With L = 40 and A = 16, one actual return pays refund 20 and handling 4, and recovers
inventory 10. Revenue increases 20, cost of sales is 6 and selling expense 4. Three actual returns
instead pay refund 60 and handling 12, recover inventory 30 and reduce revenue by 20; cost of
sales is credited 14. If two returns recover inventory worth only 14, the inventory ceiling raises
cost of sales to 2. Unknown handling, inventory NRV or an open return window prevents final
settlement rather than supplying a zero.
9.11.2
A reversal ceiling does not supply an allocation weight
IAS 36.122–123 allocates a cash-generating unit’s reversal to assets other than goodwill, propor-
tionately to their carrying amounts, subject to the individual ceilings. Suppose assets A and B
carry 40 and 60 and goodwill is 10. The unit’s recoverable amount rises to 170, allowing a total
increase of 60. A’s individual ceiling is 45 and B’s is 150. The first proportionate allocation
would assign 24 to A and 36 to B. A can accept only 5; reallocating the remaining 19 to B gives
increases of 5 and 55. Goodwill remains unchanged.
Writing wi for the original carrying amounts and ci for the remaining headroom, the method
distributes a remaining reversal R among eligible assets by Rwi/ P
j wj, caps each amount at
ci, then redistributes any remainder among assets still below their ceilings. This terminates
when the reversal is exhausted or no headroom remains. It requires a positive sum of eligible
weights whenever a positive amount remains.
If all eligible carrying amounts are zero, the denominator is zero. Neither equal weighting nor
weighting by headroom follows from the inspected source. The specification therefore retains
an unresolved allocation with unchanged books. A different rule applies to an individual asset:
a zero carrying amount, no-impairment carrying value of 80 and recoverable amount of 120
permits an individual reversal to 80 under IAS 36.117–119. That individual calculation does
not resolve the allocation among several zero-carrying assets.
