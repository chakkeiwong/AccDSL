A conditional operating scenario should start with the actual or assumed measure and its
effective date, legal scope, affected entity, product and jurisdiction. It then needs the available
supply routes, inventory, lead times, customer demand and management response. For an
assumed import delay, those facts determine whether delivery is postponed, rerouted or lost,
and which storage, production or sourcing costs change. Recognition, payment and any later
claim must follow their own facts and treatments. The filings identify these possible channels
but supply neither a tariff rate nor a universal sales reduction. Their reporting dates also do
not establish when a particular rule arose or when these exact report versions became available.
Keeping disclosed risk, operative authority, assumed restriction and response separate lets the
model retain a useful operating question without inventing its answer.

Demand, disruption and recovery follow different paths
An open supply route is useful only if a customer still wants the product. Conversely, a
customer may be ready to buy while a supplier cannot deliver a critical component. The
operating scenario introduced above must therefore describe both demand and the ability to
serve it. Apple’s annual risk discussion connects global and regional economic conditions to
consumer spending, the financial condition of its business partners and its own access to finance.
Its separate interruption discussion describes obstacles to manufacture, delivery and service
provision (Apple Inc., 2024a, Risk Factors, printed pp. 5–6). These passages give reasons to
retain several possible operating histories behind the same reported financial totals.
Apple says that sales outside the United States account for a majority of net sales and that a
majority of its supplier facilities are outside the United States. The two statements concern
different populations. A sales region does not locate a factory, and a factory’s location does not
identify the customers whose orders depend on it. The segment definitions examined earlier
generally attribute sales by customer and retail-store location, while manufacturing purchase
obligations describe purchases from outsourcing partners and component suppliers (Apple Inc.,
2024a, Note 13, printed p. 46; Liquidity and Capital Resources, printed p. 25). Linking the two
requires the actual product, component, supplier and route relationships. Geographic labels
alone cannot create them.
The macroeconomic passage describes several possible effects of the same economic conditions.
Recession, unemployment, inflation, tighter credit, higher interest rates and currency fluctuations
can weaken consumer confidence and spending. Fiscal and monetary policy, market volatility
and changes in income or asset values can also affect demand. Suppliers, manufacturers, logistics
providers, distributors, carriers, other channel partners and developers may instead face financial
instability, difficulty obtaining credit or insolvency. Apple further identifies trade-receivable
collectibility, derivative-counterparty and financial-institution failure, limited access to new
debt, reduced liquidity and declining financial-instrument fair values. The disclosure permits
a scenario to connect these channels through common conditions; it supplies neither a joint
probability law nor a requirement that every channel deteriorate together.
