12.6
Two issued groups and their reinsurance share one balance
sheet
An insurer’s accounts contain several groups at once. A profitable group cannot lend its unearned
margin to an onerous group merely because both belong to the same company. Reinsurance
may recover part of the loss, but does so through a separately measured contract. The following
extension puts those three measurements on one balance sheet and follows their cash through
the two service years. It uses the retained AASB 17 rules for an Australian private-sector
insurer’s calendar 2024 and 2025 periods.
Start with cash and equity of 100. Group A is the preceding nonzero-rate example: premium
120, claims of 44 and 48.4 at the two year ends, initial claim present value 80, risk adjustment
10 and CSM 30. Group B receives premium 100 and expects claims of 66 and 72.6 at the same
dates. At the same 10% rate, B’s claim present value is 66/1.1 + 72.6/1.12 = 120. Its separately
supplied risk adjustment is 10, so it recognises a loss of 30 and has no CSM. After premium
receipt its remaining-coverage liability is 130, including a loss component of 30. Both groups
contain contracts issued at the beginning of 2024 and provide two equal annual coverage units.
Their different profitability requires separate groups under paragraphs 16 and 47; assume no
pricing-law exception under paragraph 20. Estimating cash flows together does not authorise
pooling their CSMs (Australian Accounting Standards Board, 2022f, paragraphs 14–28, 32–38
and 47).
The current and inception discount curves remain at the assumed 10%. Each issued group’s risk
adjustment releases 5 per year, entirely in the service result under paragraph 81. All insurance
finance enters profit or loss under paragraph 88(a). Claims are incurred and settled at each year
end, with realised claims equal to the admitted expectations on this path. Cash is uninvested.
There are no acquisition costs, investment components, taxes, currency effects, new contracts or
estimate changes. The rates, expected claims, service units and risk adjustments are supplied
conditional measurements; these calculations do not estimate them from insurer data.
Now add treaty R, entered into before B is recognised. It covers all B claims at 50%, with no
exclusions, limits, settlement delay or expected non-performance. Its premium of 70 is paid
immediately. Expected recoveries are 33 and 36.3, with present value 60. A separately supplied
transferred-risk adjustment of 5 releases 2.5 each year. Its equality to half B’s adjustment is
an assumption of this example, not a general quota-share valuation rule. Without the initial
loss-recovery adjustment, the net cost held in R’s CSM would be 70 −60 −5 = 5, and the asset
after payment would be 70.
Because R was entered into in time and covers all B’s contracts, paragraphs 66A and B119C–
B119D recognise recovery income of 30 × 0.5 = 15. The adjustment increases R’s cost CSM
to 5 + 15 = 20, so its complete remaining-coverage asset after payment is 60 + 5 + 20 = 85.
Its loss-recovery component of 15 is already within that asset. It is not another receivable.
