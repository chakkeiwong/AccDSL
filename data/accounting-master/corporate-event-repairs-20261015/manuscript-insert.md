## Inspections, surrendered rights and changed employee benefits

A replacement inspection, a smaller leased area and a pension curtailment all remove something from an existing arrangement, but they remove different economic rights or obligations. The accounting must identify what survives before measuring the new arrangement. The following methods use the retained historical EU text and synthetic pretax amounts. Actual authority, contract and actuarial facts remain necessary for application.

### Replacing the inspection component

IAS 16.14 distinguishes a major inspection required for continued operation from
the physical parts inspected. Capitalize its cost as a replacement only if the
recognition criteria hold; derecognize the remaining previous inspection cost.
That obligation persists even if the original purchase did not separately
identify an inspection component. The estimated cost of a comparable future
inspection may indicate the original component cost; it is not automatically
today's replacement expenditure or an additional historical purchase.

The component record carries asset identity, inspection cohort, original cost
allocation and evidence, availability date, residual value, consumption pattern,
useful life until the next inspection, accumulated depreciation/impairment and
the replacement event. Allocate the initial asset's existing total cost among
its components; do not increase that total while recognizing the old inspection.
Advance depreciation to the replacement date, remove the old carrying amount,
add eligible new cost and begin the new component's depreciation when available.
IAS 16.43–55 requires separate significant-component depreciation and retains
depreciation during idle periods unless the applicable consumption method gives
zero. Ordinary servicing is expense under IAS 16.12, not a new inspection asset.
Other production-cost rules may place depreciation in inventory; these examples
assume a non-production use, so charges enter profit.

Suppose original equipment cost 120 includes an inspection component 20. The
remaining physical part costs 100, has ten-year straight-line life and zero
residual; the inspection has four-year life and zero residual. After two full
years the physical part carries 80 and the old inspection 10. A qualifying
inspection performed early costs 24 and is available immediately for four years.
Derecognize the old 10 as loss, capitalize 24, and retain physical carrying amount
80. The equipment now carries 104. One subsequent full year charges 10 physical
and 6 inspection depreciation, leaving 88. Expensing all 24, retaining both
inspection cohorts, or depreciating 104 uniformly over the physical life would
compute different targets.

With opening cash 100 and equipment 90, equity 190, replacement leaves cash 76,
equipment 104 and equity 180. Subsequent depreciation 16 leaves equity 164 and
equipment 88. An otherwise separate routine service costing 3 reduces cash and
profit by 3 and leaves equipment unchanged. An unavailable old-cost allocation
does not justify writing off zero: reconstruct a supported component estimate
or retain the unresolved derecognition amount. Revaluation/impairment histories
must first update the component carrying amounts under their own methods.

### Reducing a lease and paying usage-dependent rent

IFRS 16.44 first tests whether an added right and commensurate consideration make
a separate lease. A scope reduction cannot meet that increase-in-scope test.
For a modification that is not separate, paragraph45 requires revised contract
allocation, term and discount rate at the effective date. Paragraph46(a)
requires right-of-use derecognition and the gain/loss for a partial or full
termination; the remaining liability remeasurement adjusts the surviving
right-of-use asset. A mere decrease in price with unchanged scope belongs to
paragraph46(b), not automatic derecognition.

Represent the terminated rights explicitly, including the physical/use/time
units and their carrying-cost allocation. In a homogeneous space lease with an
unchanged remaining term and proportional rights, let fraction q of the rights
be surrendered. The illustrative proportional allocation removes qA of the
right-of-use carrying amount A and qL of the pre-modification liability L; the
termination gain is q(L−A). This allocation requires the homogeneous-rights
facts. Do not use the payment reduction as q or apply floor-area proportions to
heterogeneous equipment or a pure term change without a supported allocation.

For A=80 and L=100, surrender 25% of identical space with no termination fee.
Remove right-of-use 20 and liability 25; recognize gain 5. The remaining A=60
and L=75 are then remeasured. Suppose the remaining contract requires one
payment 70.4 after one year and the newly supported discount rate is 10%.
Its present value is 64. Reduce the remaining liability by 11 and the asset by
11, giving asset 49 and liability 64. The complete journal debits liability36,
credits the asset31 and credits gain5. It is wrong to send the entire liability
reduction36 to income or to omit the termination gain by adjusting the asset36.

With opening cash120/ROU80/liability100/equity100, the event leaves cash120,
ROU49/liability64/equity105. Over the one remaining year, zero residual and
straight-line use give depreciation49, interest6.4 and payment70.4. Closing cash
and equity are49.6, with no lease balances. Present-value calculation and the
right-of-use consumption pattern are independent assumptions.

A separate fee of 2 per actual unit of usage, not index/rate dependent, not
in-substance fixed and not already in the liability, is recognized under
IFRS 16.38(b) when the condition occurs. Three units create expense6/payable6;
payment reduces cash/payable6. It is not a lease-liability remeasurement.
Qualifying production use can instead include that cost in another asset under
the applicable standard. Missing usage evidence, uncertain payment character,
heterogeneous surrendered rights or a termination penalty require their own
measurement before the conditional example can be applied.

### Curtailment, settlement and the asset ceiling

The benefits record separates obligation D, fair-valued plan assets P, asset-
ceiling adjustment, recognized net liability/asset, actuarial assumptions,
remaining-year service cost, discount rate, legal discharge and actual payments.
For a liability D−P is positive. If there is a surplus, the recognized asset is
limited to the admissible economic benefit C; the added ceiling adjustment is
max(P−D−C,0). This simple representation assumes no further minimum-funding
adjustment; any applicable IFRIC 14 obligation remains a separate admission
dependency. The example supplies C, not a method for inferring legal recoverability.

IAS 19.99 requires current assumptions and asset fair values immediately before
and after amendment, curtailment or settlement. First recognize pre-event
remeasurements, then the change in benefits/covered employees, then any later
settlement. Past service cost arises from an amendment or curtailment and is
recognized at the earlier date specified by paragraph103; a related restructuring
or termination-benefit recognition can therefore precede the formal change.
The examples have no such earlier trigger. A settlement discharges obligations;
ordinary scheduled benefits included in assumptions are not a settlement gain
event. Buying insurance while retaining an obligation is also not settlement
under paragraphs111–112.

Measure past service and settlement gain/loss without the asset ceiling, then
remeasure the ceiling under paragraph101A. Service cost and settlement enter
profit unless eligible capitalization applies; actuarial and ceiling
remeasurements enter OCI under paragraph120 and are not later recycled under
paragraph122. For the rest of the year, use updated service assumptions and the
post-event net position/discount rate, allowing for subsequent contributions and
benefits, under paragraphs122A–126. An unchanged beginning-of-year interest
base would miss this required reset.

Consider opening D=1,000/P=900, net liability100 and company cash500/equity400.
At the event date, current measurement gives D=1,100/P=920: actuarial loss100
less asset gain20 creates OCI loss80 and liability180. A curtailment reduces
the freshly measured obligation by150 to950. Recognize past-service gain150,
leaving net liability30. A later transaction fully discharges a measured
obligation200 for plan assets170 plus company cash20. Settlement price190
gives gain10; D falls to750, P to750, liability to zero and cash to480.
Profit160 and OCI−80 reconcile equity from400 to480.

For the remaining half-year, assume current service cost30, annual discount
rate5%, simple half-year interest accrual and no contributions or benefit
payments. Gross obligation and asset interest are both18.75, so net interest
is zero. Closing D=798.75/P=768.75, net liability30, cash480 and equity450.
Full period profit130 and OCI−80 reconcile the change50. Using the original
net liability100 for half-year interest would incorrectly add2.5 expense.
The simple accrual convention is an illustrative assumption, not a universal
actuarial compounding rule.

To see the ceiling ordering, take D100/P130 and recoverable economic benefit10.
The surplus30 yields recognized asset10 and ceiling adjustment20. An amendment
reduces D by20 to80, while current evidence changes C to15. Recognize past-service
gain20 without the ceiling. The new surplus50 has ceiling adjustment35, so its
increase15 is an OCI loss. The net recognized asset rises only5 to15. Recording
just gain5 would misclassify profit and OCI even though closing equity agrees.

### Multi-employer information is a classification decision

IAS 19.32–36 requires classification from actual plan terms, including constructive
obligations. With adequate reliable allocation, a participant in a defined-benefit
multi-employer plan recognizes its share of obligation, assets and cost. If the
specific paragraph34 information condition holds, account as if defined
contribution and retain the paragraph148 disclosures. Insufficient information
is not proof that the plan economically has no defined-benefit exposure.

Paragraph37 separately requires any contractual surplus/deficit-funding asset or
liability and resulting profit effect. A synthetic agreed deficit payment121
due in two years, with a supplied appropriate discount rate10%, has liability100
and expense100 initially, followed by interest10 and11 and settlement121.
Ordinary current-service contributions are distinct. Do not omit the deficit
contract because ongoing contributions use defined-contribution accounting or
recognize the nominal121 immediately while also accruing interest.

Withdrawal or wind-up liabilities follow IAS 37 under paragraph39. A plan among
entities under common control is not a multi-employer plan under paragraph40;
paragraph41's plan-wide information and separate-entity charge/allocation rules
must be applied instead. The record must therefore retain employer relationships,
allocation reliability, contributions, deficit agreements, withdrawal terms and
required disclosures. Unknown relationship or contractual terms block the
classification, not the preservation of the underlying evidence.
