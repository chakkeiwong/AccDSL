The same reports state that the parent depends on subsidiary cash flows to pay its securities.
The bank’s dividends are subject to regulatory restrictions, and the IHC cannot pay dividends
or extend credit to the parent when stated capital or liquidity thresholds, or management and
board limits, apply. This means that consolidated cash, a subsidiary’s assets and the parent’s
distributable funding are different state variables. The example gives the simulator a place
to record the legal entity, guarantee and conditional funding path before it assigns a cash
movement; it does not infer a balance or a probability of default.
Note 33 makes the funding distinction measurable for 2025. Its parent-only income statement
reports net income of US$57,048m. The parent-only cash-flow statement subtracts US$71,163m
of subsidiary and affiliate net income, reports a US$14,115m parent company net loss in that
reconciliation, then adds US$50,000m of cash dividends from subsidiaries and US$8,583m of
other operating adjustments to reach US$44,468m of operating cash. Neither the income figure
nor the dividend alone is the period’s ending cash.
At 31 December 2025, the parent reports US$39m of cash and due from banks and US$9,751m
of deposits with banking subsidiaries, together US$9,790m. The cash-flow footnote says this
measure includes US$8.0bn of intercompany time deposits. The same parent-only balance sheet
reports US$669,449m of equity-method investments in bank and bank-holding subsidiaries; that
asset is not parent cash. Operating cash of US$44,468m and investing cash of US$34m, less
US$44,512m used in financing, leave closing cash and deposits US$10m below their opening
balance. These dated amounts distinguish accounting income, existing cash and conditional
subsidiary transfers. A future debt-service calculation still needs payment dates, permissible
transfers, deposit terms and funding actions (JPMorgan Chase & Co., 2026, pp. 19, 310–311).
Note 26 supplies a dated transfer capacity, which differs from both cash held and cash already
received. It reports that, at 1 January 2026, JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A. could pay approxi-
mately US$30.1bn in dividends to the parent without prior approval from its relevant banking
regulators. That is a capacity of the named bank at that date; the US$50,000m above is cash
received from subsidiaries during 2025. The note also describes regulators’ power to prohibit
unsafe or unsound dividends and the IHC’s separate capital, liquidity and management restric-
tions. It says the bank’s 2026 earnings will supplement dividend capacity, without estimating
those earnings. Turning the reported capacity into a future cash receipt still requires a dividend
decision, payment date and the applicable conditions (JPMorgan Chase & Co., 2026, pp. 4,
292).
The same note reports US$29.0bn of consolidated restricted cash at 31 December 2025:
US$19.4bn segregated for securities and cleared-derivative customers, plus US$9.6bn of non-US
central-bank reserves and cash held for other general purposes. Its balance-sheet footnote
allocates the total to US$27.8bn of deposits with banks and US$1.2bn of cash and due from
banks. Those classifications describe the same restricted-cash total. Subtracting it from the
parent’s US$9,790m would mix consolidated and parent-only perimeters and cannot determine
the parent’s unrestricted cash. Other pledged cash and securities are disclosed separately;
their category overlap must be established before adding them to a restricted-resource total
(JPMorgan Chase & Co., 2026, p. 292).
