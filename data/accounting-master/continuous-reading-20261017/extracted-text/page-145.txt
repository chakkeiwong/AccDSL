A second synthetic company owns two administrative machines, A and B, which form the entire
class for which it elects the revaluation model. They became available on 1 January 2023 at
costs 100 and 40, with useful lives of five and four years, zero residuals and even use. Cash after
their acquisition was 60, funded by historical capital of 200. Every fair value used below is an
explicitly assumed reliable measurement. Paragraphs 29, 31 and 36–38 require the class policy,
sufficiently regular measurements and coverage of the whole class; they do not infer missing
fair values from the ledger. No additional impairment is assumed after the separate assessment
required by paragraph 63.
The 2023 history establishes the opening for the 2024 statement comparison. After depreciation
of 20 and 10, A and B carry 80 and 30. At 31 December 2023 their assumed fair values are
120 and 24. A increases by 40, recorded in OCI and accumulated in its revaluation surplus. B
decreases by 6; with no surplus of its own, that decrease is expense in profit or loss. The net
class increase of 34 therefore cannot all be OCI. Such an entry would omit B’s expense of 6 and
understate OCI by the same amount while leaving total equity unchanged.
For clarity, this company chooses paragraph 35(b)’s treatment of accumulated depreciation.
At each revaluation it first debits accumulated depreciation and credits gross PPE by the
accumulated amount, leaving the net amount as the adjusted gross balance. It then posts
the revaluation difference. The prior gross cost, accumulated charge and original-cost history
remain in the asset register. This choice changes neither the net revaluation nor its allocation
between profit and OCI.
The opening 2024 balances are consequently cash 60, PPE 120 + 24 = 144, capital 200, retained
earnings −36 from depreciation and B’s loss, and surplus 40. Assets and equity both equal
204. The register also remembers B’s unreversed profit-or-loss decrease of 6. This prior-year
derivation uses the retained AASB 116 scope; it does not assert a 2023 cash-flow presentation
under the later AASB 107 compilation.
Let Li denote an asset’s outstanding earlier revaluation decrease in profit or loss, and Si its
remaining surplus credit. For an increase ui > 0, the part reversing that earlier loss goes to
profit; the rest goes to OCI. For a decrease di > 0, existing surplus absorbs the reduction
through OCI before any excess reaches profit. The operative amounts are
profit gaini = min(ui, Li),
OCI increasei = ui −min(ui, Li),
OCI decreasei = min(di, Si),
profit lossi = di −min(di, Si).
An increase reduces Li by its profit gain and adds its OCI increase to Si. A decrease reduces
Si by its OCI decrease and adds its profit loss to Li. These are revaluation-history rules; the
separate impairment reversal ceiling is considered in the impairment method. No surplus is
transferred during use in this example. If that permitted transfer were elected, the later surplus
available to absorb a decrease would be smaller.
For 2024, the revalued amounts are depreciated over the remaining four and three years. The
charges are 120/4 = 30 and 24/3 = 8, leaving carrying amounts 90 and 16. The company then
revalues the whole class to 45 and 22. A falls by 45: its surplus absorbs 40 through OCI and
the remaining 5 is a profit-or-loss expense. B rises by 6, reversing its own earlier loss through
profit. After eliminating accumulated depreciation, gross PPE is 45 and 22, with surplus zero.
Profit is −38 −5 + 6 = −37 and OCI is −40. Cash 60 plus PPE 67 equals closing equity 127.
A now retains a prior revaluation loss of 5; B retains none.
