10.10
Changed conversion terms change the accounting question
A maturity conversion, an early repurchase and an inducement to convert all remove or alter
debt, but they do not allocate the resulting consideration in the same way. The issuer must retain
the old conversion rights, the revised rights, the date of amendment and the actual settlement. A
record that says only converted loses the facts needed to distinguish these events. The following
cases use the retained Australian standards for explicit calendar-2024/2025 for-profit Tier 1
illustrations, without optional later amendments. They are pretax: tax consequences remain
uncomputed.
For an early repurchase with unchanged conversion privileges, the issuer allocates the considera-
tion between the liability and equity components using the method of the original separation.
The liability allocation uses its current fair value; the difference from its carrying amount enters
profit or loss, while the equity allocation stays in equity (Australian Accounting Standards
Board, 2022c, AG33–34 and IE39–46). The standard’s example has a face amount of 1,000,
half-yearly coupons of 50 and ten payments remaining. The original half-year discount rate is
5.5%; the contemporaneous comparable nonconvertible debt rate is 4%. For either rate r, the
remaining debt value is

The first term values the ten coupons; the second values principal at the last payment. The
source rounds the carrying and current fair values to 962 and 1,081. Repurchase consideration
of 1,700 then allocates 619 to equity and produces debt settlement expense of 119. The entries
debit debt 962, expense 119 and equity 619, and credit cash 1,700. Expensing the whole
1,700 −962 = 738 would misclassify the equity purchase. The original equity component of 60
remains within equity; it does not cap the 619 deduction. Exact present-value fractions and the
source’s rounded presentation remain separate in the accompanying calculations.
An inducement asks a different valuation question. At the amendment date, compare the fair
value of what the holder receives under the revised conversion terms with what the holder
would have received under the original terms. Their difference is an inducement loss (Australian
Accounting Standards Board, 2022c, AG35 and IE47–50). In the retained example, changing
the conversion price from 25 to 20 on face amount 1,000 increases shares from 40 to 50. At
the amendment-date share quotation of 40, the additional ten shares are worth 400. A later
quotation of 50 would give 500 and would use the wrong measurement date.
For an explicitly immediate, unconditional share-only conversion, suppose the liability’s admitted
carrying amount is 960 and the original equity component is 60. This carrying amount is a
separate illustrative input. The resulting entry debits debt 960 and inducement expense 400,
and credits issued equity 1,360; 50 shares are issued. Original conversion equity remains in
equity. The 400 expense offsets the corresponding part of the issued equity in total equity,
while derecognizing the debt increases net equity by 960. There is no cash movement. Noncash
financing disclosure preserves the event without inventing a financing receipt (Australian
Accounting Standards Board, 2023a, 43–44).
This immediate case does not determine the recognition timing of a delayed, contingent or
cash inducement. Those terms must first establish the obligation. Nor does an ability to
settle in shares establish equity classification. A derivative with a net-cash alternative fails
the all-equity-alternatives condition, and an obligation to deliver a variable number of shares
worth a fixed sum does not represent a residual ownership interest (Australian Accounting
Standards Board, 2022c, 21 and 26–27). The system must refuse the fixed-for-fixed method
when these facts are missing or contradicted, while retaining the instrument and its unresolved
classification.
