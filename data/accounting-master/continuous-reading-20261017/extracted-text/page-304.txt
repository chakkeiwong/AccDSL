12.16
What a bootstrap must reproduce
Separating fitted means from future payments is necessary, but it still leaves the statistical
target to be chosen. An insurer knows an observed claims triangle. A method assessment
may instead hold only its latest diagonal fixed, allowing different earlier histories to lead to
those same cumulative amounts. These are different conditioning questions. Let D denote the
observed triangle, Q its latest diagonal, R the future reserve payment and bR(D) the estimate
calculated from the triangle. A distribution of R −bR(D) conditional on Q allows the earlier
entries of D, and therefore the estimate, to vary. Conditional on the whole D, that particular
estimate is fixed.
A small authored example makes the distinction concrete. Suppose two earlier histories are
equally likely given the same Q and produce estimates of 90 and 110. Let future payments
be 80 or 120 with equal probability, independent of those histories conditional on Q. The
prediction error given Q is then −30, −10, 10, 30, each with probability 1/4: its mean is zero
and variance 500. Fixing the history whose estimate is 90 instead gives errors −10, 30, mean
10 and variance 400. Both calculations are correct for their stated conditioning information.
They cannot serve as interchangeable validation targets. Nor does holding an estimate fixed
imply that all uncertainty about model parameters has disappeared; a Bayesian parameter
distribution, for example, would be a further explicitly specified model.
Steinmetz and Jentsch analyze a Mack bootstrap against the prediction error conditional on
the latest diagonal. Their asymptotic construction adds older accident years and additional
development observations while retaining the current calendar boundary. It does not obtain
more information merely by increasing the number of bootstrap draws. The framework assumes
positive initial cumulative claims with finite moments, specified independence, development
factors tending to one, sufficiently fast decay of development variances, and further moment and
regularity conditions. It is distinct from the incremental overdispersed-Poisson procedure in the
preceding section (Steinmetz and Jentsch, 2024, Sections 2–4, especially Assumptions 2.2–2.5).
Their analysis separates an unscaled process term from a smaller estimation term. Under
the stated assumptions, the process term has a nondegenerate, generally non-Gaussian limit.
Correctly reproducing its distribution requires the correct conditional family for future devel-
opment factors; matching two moments within a wrongly chosen family is insufficient. The
original bootstrap can reproduce that limit while missing the conditional mean and covariance
of the estimation term at its own, square-root sample-size scale (Steinmetz and Jentsch, 2024,
Assumptions 4.6–4.9, Theorems 4.8–4.11 and Remark 4.12).
There is no contradiction in these two findings. For illustration, let a process term be an equally
likely −1 or 1, and an independent estimation term an equally likely −1/n or 1/n. Their sum
has variance 1 + 1/n2. Dropping the estimation term produces the same limiting distribution
for the unscaled sum, yet multiplying that omitted term by n would have left variance one.
This example explains the distinction between scales; it is not a derivation of the paper’s Mack
limit or evidence that a finite insurer triangle is adequately approximated by it.
