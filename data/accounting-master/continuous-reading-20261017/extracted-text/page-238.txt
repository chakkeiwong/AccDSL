Eliminating the loan, retaining the currency exposure
An intragroup loan disappears from the consolidated balance sheet, yet its exchange gain can
remain in group profit. The reason becomes visible when we follow both the loan and the
money it financed. IAS 21.45 requires the exchange result to survive elimination; paragraph 32
determines when it belongs in other comprehensive income instead (European Commission,
2023a, IAS 21.32–33, .45).
Consider a parent whose functional and presentation currency is USD and a wholly owned
subsidiary whose functional currency is EUR. Control and both functional currencies are
established assumptions. At an opening rate of USD1.20 per EUR, the parent’s balances are
cash 56, an investment in the subsidiary carried at historical cost 24, and a EUR100 loan
receivable worth 120. Its equity is therefore 200. The subsidiary holds EUR120 in cash, owes
EUR100 to the parent and has EUR20 of paid-in equity. At the opening rate, its equity matches
the parent’s investment. Eliminating that investment and the loan leaves group cash and equity
of 200.
We isolate an exchange-rate remeasurement of these established balances, holding every other
measurement input fixed. This is neither the initial valuation of a new loan nor a complete
reporting period. Interest, tax, impairment, hedges and new transactions are outside this
calculation; IAS 21.24 leaves the underlying carrying amount to the relevant measurement
standards. Thus EUR100 is a given monetary carrying amount, not an assumption that an
interest-free long-term loan must be recognised at its face value (European Commission, 2023a,
IAS 21.23–24, .50). All following statement amounts are in USD unless explicitly marked EUR.
Now let the rate rise to USD1.30 per EUR. The parent remeasures its receivable from 120 to 130:
debit the receivable 10 and credit exchange gain 10. The subsidiary records no exchange gain on
its EUR loan in its own EUR books. For group presentation, its cash becomes 120 × 1.30 = 156
and its payable becomes 100 × 1.30 = 130. Its historical capital remains 24. The remaining 2 is
translation OCI, explained by its EUR20 net assets and the rate change: 20 × (1.30 −1.20) = 2
(European Commission, 2023a, IAS 21.28, .33, .39–41).
Consolidation now debits the translated loan payable 130 and credits the parent’s receivable
130. It also debits the subsidiary’s historical capital 24 and credits the parent’s investment 24.
These entries eliminate the internal balances; neither entry removes the parent’s exchange gain.
Group cash is 56 + 156 = 212, with no external liability in this example. Opening equity 200,
exchange profit 10 and translation OCI 2 reconcile to the same 212. Deleting the profit merely
because the receivable was internal would leave equity short by 10. The group still owns the
euros whose dollar value has risen (European Commission, 2023a, IFRS 10.B86; IAS 21.45).
