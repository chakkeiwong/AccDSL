Consider two explicitly synthetic histories. In the first, twelve years was a reasonable useful life
at acquisition and at both year-end reviews. A newly enacted operating restriction on 1 April
2025 now limits use to the end of 2028. That restriction neither existed nor was anticipated at
the earlier dates. In the second history, the reliable, approved schedule at acquisition already
specified six years, but a transcription error entered twelve. The original schedule was available
and could reasonably have been obtained and considered at each authorization. Six years
remains appropriate; the new discovery exposes the old mistake. These histories differ in facts,
not in an accounting election available on the same known evidence.
AASB 108.5 makes that availability condition part of the definition of a prior-period error.
Paragraphs 32–34A distinguish new information and developments from correction of an error;
paragraphs 36–38 apply estimate changes to the current and relevant future periods (Australian
Accounting Standards Board, 2022a). AASB 116.50–51 and 60–62 require systematic allocation,
review of useful life and a method reflecting consumption (Australian Accounting Standards
Board, 2022b). Here there is one component, even consumption, no capitalization into another
asset, and an independently satisfied recoverability assessment. The shorter life does not replace
that assessment.
Dates determine which accounts are being corrected. The 2024 statements were authorized on 31
March 2025; the discovery occurs the following day. The 2023 statements were authorized on 31
March 2024. No 2025 interim statements have been issued. Thus the error affects two authorized
annual reports and an unissued current quarter. Moving discovery before the 2024 authorization
would change the treatment of that report under paragraph 41. The example concerns newly
arising April conditions in the estimate history; it makes no separate determination about
events revealing conditions that existed at the earlier reporting date.
The historical method comparison uses the retained December 2022 AASB 108 and 116 compi-
lations and September 2023 AASB 112 compilation. It does not establish a later amendment
chain or actual-company applicability. Assume a private, for-profit Tier 1 entity with no publicly
traded ordinary or potential ordinary shares, no filing for public issuance and no voluntary
EPS presentation. Those facts place this illustration outside the inspected AASB 133.2–3 scope
(Australian Accounting Standards Board, 2021b). A public or voluntarily presenting entity
needs the EPS restatement as well; the financing example’s share schedule cannot simply be
borrowed for this company.
New information changes the remaining allocation
Use equal monthly depreciation. In the estimate history the first three months of 2025 still
consume 10×3/12 = 2.5, leaving 97.5 on 1 April. There are 45 months from April 2025 through
December 2028. The remaining carrying amount, rather than original cost or a reconstructed
earlier estimate, is allocated across those months:
dApr−Dec 2025 = 97.5
45 × 9 = 19.5,
d2025 = 2.5 + 19.5 = 22.
Year-end PPE is 100 −22 = 78. Each full year from 2026 through 2028 has depreciation
97.5 × 12/45 = 26; the future allocations 19.5 + 26 + 26 + 26 = 97.5 exhaust the April balance.
The reported 2023 and 2024 depreciation remains 10 in each year. Revising those amounts to
20 would turn later information into a fact supposedly known earlier.
