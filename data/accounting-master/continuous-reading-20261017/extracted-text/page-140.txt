Revalued impairment and its related deferred tax
IAS 36.60–64 (European Commission, 2023a, IAS 36.60–64,117–124; IAS 12.61A–65) treats a
revalued asset’s impairment as a revaluation decrease: charge the same asset’s existing surplus
through OCI before charging profit. Afterward, determine deferred tax from the revised carrying
amount and tax base. IAS 12.61A attaches current and deferred tax to the related item even
when the item was recognized in a different period. Preserve the origin of each temporary
difference so a later tax-rate change does not move all tax to profit by default. Where exact
attribution is exceptionally impracticable, paragraph 63 permits a reasonable allocation or a
more appropriate method; document that circumstance.
Take an asset carrying 140, tax base 100, gross same-asset revaluation surplus 40 and related
deferred tax liability 10 at a supplied 25% rate. There is no passage of time or tax-base
movement. Recoverable amount 90 causes loss 50: OCI 40 and profit 10. The old DTL 10
reverses in OCI. A deductible difference 10 gives DTA 2.5 in profit only if recovery is probable;
that condition is supplied here. Net OCI loss 30 plus net profit loss 7.5 equals the net-asset
decrease 37.5, from 140 −10 = 130 to 90 + 2.5 = 92.5. Offset presentation is not assumed: DTA
and DTL eligibility must follow the applicable jurisdictional and legal-offset rules.
In the same no-time-passage illustration, recoverable amount later recovers to 130.
IAS
36.117–120 limits reversal to the no-impairment amount and reverses the prior profit loss 10
first, with the remaining 30 through OCI. Reversing DTA 2.5 charges profit; establishing DTL
7.5 charges OCI. Net profit gain 7.5 and OCI gain 22.5 sum to 30, the increase from 92.5 to
122.5. A revaluation increase beyond the no-impairment amount is a separate IAS 16 event, not
unlimited reversal; future depreciation must update both the reversal cap and carrying amount.
Separately, if the original carrying 140/tax base 100 asset’s admitted future tax rate changes
from 25% to 30% before any impairment, DTL rises from 10 to 12. The additional tax 2
relates to the earlier revaluation and is charged to OCI, not profit. The example assumes
enacted/substantively enacted rates and the same manner of recovery; it does not infer tax law
or settlement method from the accounting carrying value.
Acquisition and investment tax are separate questions
IAS 12.66 (European Commission, 2023a, IAS 12.39–51,66–68) requires acquisition-date tem-
porary differences on identifiable assets and liabilities to enter acquisition accounting, subject
to recognition rules. For a simple full acquisition paying 150, identifiable fair-valued assets
150 with tax base 100 and an admitted 25% rate create DTL 12.5. Net identifiable assets
137.5 give goodwill 12.5. There is no recursive DTL on the initial recognition of that goodwill
under paragraph 15(a). This exception is not permission to omit the DTL on the identifiable
asset. The treatment of an acquirer’s own previously unrecognized DTA, and acquiree benefits
recognized later, follows paragraphs 67–68; the former does not automatically change acquisition
goodwill.
Outside-basis differences on investments are tracked separately from temporary differences
inside the subsidiary’s asset book. Under IAS 12.39, a taxable difference avoids DTL only to the
extent the investor controls reversal timing and reversal is probably absent in the foreseeable
future. Both conditions are required. For a taxable difference 60 and supplied 20% tax on
the expected reversal, DTL is 12 when the exception does not hold; it is not recognized where
both conditions demonstrably hold. Unknown control or distribution intent is unresolved, not
evidence for the exception. An associate investor usually cannot infer control from a minority
shareholding. Under paragraph 44, a deductible difference requires probable foreseeable reversal
