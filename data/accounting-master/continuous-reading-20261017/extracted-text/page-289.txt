A small settlement example shows why the guarantee matters even when the stated share is
fixed. Consider only a maturity-benefit component, in one currency at one date. Assume a held
pool allocated to this component has amount U, and the policyholder is owed the larger of
90 and 0.9U. These are illustrative contractual choices. The amount left from the pool after
payment is U −max(90, 0.9U). Writing the payment as 0.9U + max(90 −0.9U, 0) shows that
the amount left is 0.1U −max(90 −0.9U, 0): the nominal retained share less the extra payment
needed to meet the minimum.

Moving from 80 to 100 leaves 20 more after payment: the ordinary share rises by 2 and the extra
floor payment falls by 18. Moving from 100 to 120 leaves only 2 more because the minimum
no longer binds. The negative amount at 80 is a funding shortfall for this component. These
unweighted settlement outcomes explain the exposure; they do not establish insurance-contract
eligibility, a present value or the insurer’s profit. Other benefits and costs remain outside this
component example, and its held-pool assumption does not replace the standard’s separate
accounting for investment assets (Australian Accounting Standards Board, 2022f, paragraphs
B66(a), B107–B108).
The accounting calculation therefore needs more than this payoff schedule. Paragraph B104
defines the variable fee as the entity’s share of underlying fair value less fulfilment cash flows
that do not vary based on underlying returns. Changes in the obligation to pass through the
underlying fair value do not adjust CSM, whereas the entity-share changes relate to future service.
Financial-guarantee changes have their own future-service treatment under B113(b). Paragraph
45 retains the loss-component and risk-mitigation exceptions, as well as new-contract, currency
and service-allocation movements. Applying those rules requires the group’s measured cash flows,
discount and risk adjustments, remaining CSM and loss component, service information and
any eligible mitigation designation. The original increase of 2 remains a valid share-component
calculation; the complete CSM movement needs these additional inputs (Australian Accounting
Standards Board, 2022f, paragraphs 32–38, 45, 48–50 and B104–B118).
12.9
Pricing the floor before releasing the margin
The settlement schedule identifies a liability that increases when the underlying assets fall. To
measure that liability before settlement, we need a financial model. A small, explicitly specified
market makes the distinction between a price and a forecast visible. The retained standard
permits a replicating portfolio to measure cash flows that it matches in amount, timing and
uncertainty; other techniques must remain consistent with observable market prices (Australian
Accounting Standards Board, 2022f, paragraphs B44–B48).
Suppose a non-dividend-paying traded asset costs S0 = 100. Its next observed value is either
Su = 120 or Sd = 80, after which it remains at that value until payment. Cash earns zero
interest, so its accumulation factor is R = 1. There are exactly two financial states, unrestricted
borrowing and shorting, no transaction costs and no counterparty failure. These are assumptions
of the illustration. The financial observation precedes all supplied coverage units; the subsequent
service interval contains the mortality risk. An actual period with service already provided
would require its own dated allocation.
