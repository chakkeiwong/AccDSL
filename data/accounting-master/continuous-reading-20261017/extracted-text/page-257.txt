11.3.1
The same price decline can reach profit and OCI differently
Consider a debt asset purchased for 100 and now worth 98. Supply cumulative ECL of 3, with
no interest accrual, foreign-exchange movement, tax or fees in this measurement comparison.
Opening cash and equity are each 200; purchase leaves cash 100. The three cases below have
different admitted business models or an eligible FVTPL designation. They compare reporting
consequences, rather than three freely selectable policies for one factual case.
At amortised cost, record impairment expense and allowance 3. Gross asset 100 less allowance 3
gives net asset 97, so cash 100 plus net asset 97 equals equity 197. A subsequent derecognising
sale for 98 debits cash 98 and allowance 3, credits gross asset 100 and recognises gain 1.
Cumulative profit is −3 + 1 = −2, and closing cash and equity are 198.
For debt FVOCI, the balance-sheet asset is 98.
Record the fair-value decline 2 in OCI.
Impairment expense 3 enters profit, with its allowance counterpart credited to OCI rather than
deducted again from the asset’s fair value. Net OCI is therefore −2 + 3 = 1; profit is −3, and
total comprehensive income is −2. Cash 100 plus asset 98 equals equity 198. Reporting asset 95
by subtracting the allowance once more would count the credit effect twice. On a derecognising
sale for 98, remove the asset and reclassify cumulative OCI gain 1 to profit. Cumulative profit
then equals −2, with OCI cleared (European Commission, 2023a, IFRS 9.5.5.1–2, 5.7.10–11).
Under FVTPL, the decline from 100 to 98 is expense 2. There is no separate IFRS 9 loss
allowance added to that fair-value loss. The sale at the same price creates no further gain. All
three examples ultimately close with cash and equity 198, but their interim carrying amounts
and placement of income differ. Reconciliation must preserve the chosen route rather than infer
it from the equal final cash.
An equity investment is a distinct instrument. Where the irrevocable FVOCI election is available
and made at initial recognition, a fall from 100 to 98 gives OCI loss 2 and no fair-value expense
in profit. It excludes an investment held for trading and specified contingent consideration of
an acquirer in a business combination. On sale, the cumulative loss is not recycled to profit;
a transfer within equity is permitted. Dividends follow their separate recognition conditions,
including whether they clearly represent recovery of part of the investment’s cost. With no
dividends in this example, cash and equity again end at 198 while cumulative profit is zero
(European Commission, 2023a, IFRS 9.5.7.5–6, B5.7.1–1A). Copying the debt recycling entry
into this case would wrongly create expense 2 on sale.
11.3.2
A familiar interest label can conceal a different exposure
A floating rate need not faithfully represent the passage of time. Suppose principal 100 pays
monthly interest but resets each month to a one-year rate. A comparison instrument would reset
monthly to a one-month rate, with the other relevant terms and credit quality matched. In one
supplied six-month scenario, hold the contractual annualised rate at 12% and the benchmark
rate at 6%. Each month’s interest is respectively 1 and 0.5; six payments differ by 3. With
principal repaid at month six, total undiscounted receipts are 106 and 103.
This calculation identifies the difference to investigate. IFRS 9’s modified-time-value test
compares undiscounted contractual and benchmark cash flows both by reporting period and
cumulatively over the instrument’s life, considering reasonably possible scenarios. A current
yield curve, discounted present-value match or an invented percentage cutoff cannot settle
whether the difference could be significant. Retain the benchmark construction, scenarios,
period totals and significance reasoning before making that finding (European Commission,
2023a, IFRS 9.B4.1.9A–9E).
