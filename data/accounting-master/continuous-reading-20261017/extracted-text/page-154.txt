9.5.4
An offset must explain the individual rows
The required offset can be tested without pretending that its transaction has been observed.
Suppose a separate economic obligation would justify an expense and an unpaid liability of
q in History 1 and q −1,000 in History 2, with q ≥1,000. These are alternative amounts in
two histories; they are not a release booked within either history. For a numerical example,
take q = 2,000: each branch debits expense and credits its own liability, for 2,000 or 1,000
respectively. The difference supplies the 1,000 lower liability and 1,000 higher profit required of
History 2’s remainder. The economic obligation is an additional premise. Subtracting reported
totals has not established that it exists.
Even conditional on that premise, a matched total is insufficient. Assume both the retirement
loss and the new expense enter product cost, and both the capital payable and the new liability
enter the same non-tax current liability category. History 2’s extra loss then offsets its smaller
expense within that expense row, and its larger capital payable offsets its smaller expense
liability within that liability row. Other operations, distributions, share counts and OCI remain
fixed in this comparison. Equal tax expense and tax balances are a further condition: equal
pretax profit does not imply equal deductions, tax bases or deferred tax.
The indirect cash-flow statement exposes another restriction. If the expense liability is treated as
an ordinary operating accrual, its 1,000 smaller increase reduces the working-liability adjustment
by 1,000. The extra retirement loss increases the noncash addback by 1,000. Total operating
cash flow is unchanged, but the separately reported Other adjustment and Other current and
non-current liabilities adjustment each change. That construction fails to reproduce Apple’s
detailed cash-flow statement, even though it balances and matches total operating cash (Apple
Inc., 2024a, printed p. 33).
