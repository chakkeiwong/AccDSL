6.2
A certain liability need not identify its creditor
Suppose the admitted records establish exactly one payable of 100 currency units, but leave its
creditor unresolved between A and B. For this constructed example the declared family has two
histories: in hA the company owes A 100 and owes B nothing; in hB it owes B 100 and owes
A nothing. These are complete alternatives within the example, not two sampled points from
a wider family. The question does a payable exist? has the answer yes in both histories. The
question is A owed 100? does not.
Now ask for the rows of the payable schedule. If Q(h) returns those rows, then Q(hA) =
{(A, 100)} and Q(hB) = {(B, 100)}. Their intersection is empty: no creditor–amount row
occurs in both. Reading that empty table as the company’s complete schedule would wrongly
assert that it owes nothing. Reading the union as its complete schedule would instead assert
that it owes 200. Evaluating the total within each history gives 100 in both. The uncertainty
concerns the creditor, not the existence or total of the payable.
This distinction is central to Libkin’s critique of intersection-based answers. His example retains
a tuple with an unknown second component; discarding it loses knowledge that a tuple with
the stated first component exists. An intersection read under a closed-world interpretation can
also add an unsupported absence claim (Libkin, 2014, Section 3 and the opening of Section 6).
A table of individually certain rows is therefore a restricted view of knowledge, not generally a
replacement for the possible answer schedules.
For a nonempty feasible history family H = HM(Dk) and a total, deterministic query Q on its
histories, retain the family of answers
AQ(H) = {Q(h) : h ∈H}.
Each answer remains linked to the histories that produce it. Equality of payable schedules
alone does not license merging histories for another query. For a row-valued query, a separately
requested certain-tuples view is

Membership in TQ(H) asserts that a row appears in every answer. Omission asserts only that
the row is not established in every answer; it does not assert absence from every answer. A
total computed after taking this intersection answers a different question from totals computed
within histories. In the payable example, TQ(H) is empty while {total(Q(h)) : h ∈H} = {100}.

Taking P to mean that the schedule contains at least one payable proves existence without
identifying A or B as the creditor. Allowing a third history with no payable would change that
answer. The two-history result consequently depends on the admitted evidence and scope that
require the liability to exist, not merely on the two rows chosen for display.
Libkin distinguishes a logical description of knowledge shared by all answers from an object
representing their greatest lower bound in an information ordering. These representations need
not exist in the chosen language; ordinary tuple intersection is not their general definition (Libkin,
2014, Section 5.3, equations (6)–(8)). AccDSL’s answer family and Boolean propositions above
are local definitions. They do not assert that every family has a finite relational representation.
A symbolic answer must carry its conditions and preserve the stated family, or declare the
restriction or approximation it makes.
