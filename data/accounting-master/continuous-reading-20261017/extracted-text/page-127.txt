Restoration estimates change the asset and the obligation differently
The restoration record retains the related component and liability, cost/revaluation policy,
gross cost/accumulated depreciation, remaining life/residual, current cash-flow estimate and
rate, prior estimate identity, same-asset revaluation surplus/prior profit deficit, hypothetical
cost-model carrying amount, impairment evidence and whole-class valuation review. IFRIC 1.2
(European Commission, 2023a, IFRIC 1.1–8) requires the original liability to have been included
in the asset’s cost. A standalone litigation obligation is not admitted to this asset-adjustment
method.
Separate passage of time from a new estimate. Under the cost model, a liability increase adds
to PPE; a decrease reduces PPE only to zero, with excess in profit. An increase prompts
assessment of impairment indications and an IAS 36 test when required. Depreciate the adjusted
carrying amount less admitted residual over the remaining useful life. Discount unwind is
finance expense, never capitalised under IFRIC 1.8. Once useful life is exhausted, later estimate
changes go to profit under 7 regardless of asset model. Actual settlement reduces the obligation
and cash; a difference from the last carrying amount requires a dated remeasurement.
With cash 100, PPE 80 and provision 20, opening equity is 160. An admitted new provision 50
adds 30 to PPE. Recoverable amount 90 requires impairment 20: PPE 90, cash 100, liability
50 and equity 140. Four years remaining and zero residual gives annual depreciation 22.5.
The subsequent depreciation-only illustration assumes a separately supplied zero discount rate
and no further liability change; PPE 67.5 and equity 117.5 follow. In another branch, PPE
30/provision 80 and a new provision 30 require Dr provision 50 / Cr PPE 30 / Cr profit 20.
Negative PPE 20 would be wrong. Cash 100 then equals liability 30 plus equity 70.
Under the revaluation model, a liability increase first consumes a same-asset surplus through
OCI, then affects profit; a decrease reverses a same-asset profit deficit and otherwise increases
OCI. A decrease exceeding the hypothetical cost-model carrying amount has an immediate
profit component. Determine any necessary class revaluation first so that these splits use the
correct history. With PPE 150/provision 50/surplus 20, an increase 35 gives OCI loss 20 and
profit loss 15; PPE stays 150 only because a supplied class valuation review supports it. Cash
100 then gives equity 165 and liability 85. Surplus on another asset cannot be borrowed. In a
separate decrease case, PPE 25, hypothetical cost 40, earlier profit deficit 15 and provision 80 →
30 yield excess 10 plus deficit reversal 15 in profit and 25 in OCI. Cash 100+PPE 25=provision
30+equity 95. Using 25 as the cost floor would compute the wrong split. The prior deficit
explains why 25 and 40 differ; no fresh impairment is inferred from that difference.
A full cash history opens cash 100/PPE 120/provision 20/equity 200. Two-year straight-line life
and zero residual give depreciation 60 in year 1. At 10%, unwind 2 raises the provision 22; new
supported estimate 33 adds 11 to PPE. Close PPE 71/provision 33/equity 138, with pretax loss
62 and no cash change. Year 2 depreciation 71 and unwind 3.3 give loss 74.3; actual settlement
36.3 leaves cash/equity 63.7 and zero PPE/provision. Across both years depreciation 131 plus
finance 5.3 equals cumulative loss 136.3. The total cash bridge is exact; IAS 7 classification of
restoration settlement needs a separate fact-specific policy. The method therefore returns a
cash-classification obligation rather than inventing a complete categorized cash-flow statement.
A fully exhausted PPE 0 with provision 20 →25 gives expense 5 even if a historic revaluation
reserve exists.
