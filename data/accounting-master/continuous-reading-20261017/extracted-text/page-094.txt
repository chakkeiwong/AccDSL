Updating a law without counting evidence twice
Two financial histories can be compatible with the same reports without being equally probable.
Once an explicitly justified law is supplied, genuinely new evidence may change it. For finite
histories h, prior mass p(h) and an admitted observation likelihood L(D | h), Bayes’ rule gives
p(h | D) =
p(h)L(D | h)
P
g p(g)L(D | g).
The denominator is the probability of that evidence under the declared model. If it is zero, the
condition is impossible in that model; dropping incompatible histories and renormalising an
empty collection cannot repair it.
For two histories with assumed prior masses 1/3 and 2/3 and likelihoods 3/4 and 1, the
unnormalised masses are 1/4 and 2/3. Their sum is 11/12, so the posterior masses are 3/11 and
8/11. If the second history’s cash calculation fails, the first still has mass 3/11 and unresolved
cash retains mass 8/11. A report giving the successful history probability one would answer a
different, success-conditioned question.
An observation has a semantic information identity as well as a file locator. Reimporting the
same observation through another file does not create a second measurement. Repeating an
identical admitted condition is idempotent: it keeps the law and information lineage unchanged.
Submitting an already consumed observation as a fresh likelihood block is rejected as duplicate
information. Equal selected member sets alone do not prove that two conditions have the same
meaning. An admitted conditional kernel may divide a region’s existing mass when it is refined;
the number of new representatives never determines that division.
