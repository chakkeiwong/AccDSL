# Chapter 11 — credit history, capital and settlement

Exact Version 3.46, physical pages 244–277. The current-candidate ordered
segments 241–246 through 277–282 preserve the reading. This is author diagnosis;
independent reader, domain and human acceptance remain pending.

The chapter makes the bank's legal and institutional boundaries consequential
before calculating a loan. JPMorgan's parent, bank, issuer and guarantor are
distinct. Reported earnings, cash, dividend capacity, principal and carrying
amounts therefore answer different questions. Distribution limits require
event dates and specific income definitions; debt succession does not prove
repayment or derecognition. The future acquisition example similarly prevents
estimated loans at closing from becoming assets already held at the reporting
date. These examples establish why the later synthetic cases need explicit
facts rather than labels alone.

The cash path then separates origination from external settlement. Its first
prudential ratio is openly illustrative. The restricted national-bank case
adds dated rule support and shows why gross exposure, allowance-cap base,
eligible allowance and final risk-weighted assets must remain distinct.
Capital can remain adequate under the stated ratios while a deposit payment
fails. Average-assets evidence is needed separately for leverage. This pays
off the opening distinction between accounting equity, capital and cash.

Classification appears before detailed loan measurement. Business-model and
contractual-flow findings admit a route; they are not interchangeable choices
for a preferred profit figure. The amortised-cost, debt FVOCI and FVTPL cases
end at equal cash but preserve different interim reporting. Equity FVOCI
provides a useful counterexample to copying debt recycling. Modified time
value and nonrecourse terms then show why a familiar interest label cannot
replace assessment of the actual contract.

The credit-loss sequence preserves the date and meaning of each change.
Twelve-month ECL selects default events, including their later cash shortfalls.
Drawing a commitment conditions out no-draw but preserves the original credit
history. The provision-to-allowance transition exposes both a stale funding
probability and an improper staging reset, which happen to produce the same
wrong amount. The ordinary loan then separates accrual, impairment, recovery
and write-off. A balanced closing statement cannot substitute for the correct
interim interest basis.

POCI measurement adds a different initial condition: expected losses already
enter purchase measurement and the credit-adjusted rate. The signed allowance
therefore permits subsequent favorable changes; a zero floor would suppress
the gain. Interim receipts and a separately supported partial write-off extend
that mechanism without changing its original-rate principle. ACT/365F and the
proportional allocation are declared assumptions. The later rate-bracket case
also repairs a potentially misleading convenience of exactly representable
rates: numerical enclosures concern one original root, and their separate
endpoints cannot be assembled into an attainable set of journals.

Modification, credit recovery and legal release remain distinct. The same
forecast mean does not establish cure, and cure does not by itself reverse a
significant increase in credit risk. Collateral collected after maturity has
its own discount date and costs. Its accounting write-off does not waive the
unpaid legal claim or create a second bank-owned collateral asset.

The final extensions preserve the same argument across revolving capacity,
fees and transfers. Repayment can restore capacity without resetting credit
history. Fee purpose determines whether cash enters yield or service revenue.
A partial sale uses an eligible unit and relative fair values, while the same
cash receipt under retained risks can be borrowing. Guarantees, calls, puts and
collars leave different exposures. The net of recognised asset and associated
liability already represents the retained option; another option would count
the right twice. Amortised cost needs its own asset and liability schedules.

The funding ending returns to actual cash. A request creates no receipt, and a
lost acknowledgement alone proves neither settlement nor non-settlement.
Confirmed receipts of 8, 6 and 0 lead to unpaid deposits of 2, 4 and 10 while
equity remains 20. Keeping that unpaid portion inside the deposit liability
avoids double counting. This links the earlier event/replay design to a bank
obligation with real economic consequences.

No new local prose or cross-reference defect was confirmed in this chapter.
Its demanding detail remains an author observation, not evidence that a human
reader can reconstruct it unaided. The chapter distinguishes conditional
accounting calculations from current-rule applicability, actual legal facts,
credit calibration, general allocation methods and a complete bank model.
The closing policy discussion requires independent assumptions and time-safe
comparisons before forecasting claims. The insurance opening then carries the
cash-versus-income distinction into service and claim histories. All wider
obligations and not_ready remain unchanged.
