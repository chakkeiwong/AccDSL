Under FIFO process costing, retain the opening 150 and allocate current cost only to current
work. Completing the ten opening units requires five equivalent conversion units. Seventy
units are started and completed, and the closing twenty require ten equivalent conversion
units. Current conversion effort is therefore 5 + 70 + 10 = 85, at 510/85 = 6 per equivalent
unit. Current material cost is 900/90 = 10 per new unit. Completing opening work costs
150 + 5 × 6 = 180; the seventy new completed units cost 70 × (10 + 6) = 1120. Transfer 1300
from WIP to finished goods and retain 20 × 10 + 10 × 6 = 260 in WIP. Completion is not a
sale, so this transfer does not itself create cost of sales or a cash payment.
Weighted-average process costing pools the opening and current costs. Material cost remains
10 per equivalent unit, but conversion cost becomes (50 + 510)/(80 + 10) = 56/9. Completed
goods then cost 11680/9 and closing WIP 2360/9, still totaling 1560. Posting at cents gives
1297.78 and 262.22 with a preserved rounding allocation. The methods distribute the same
total cost differently because the opening conversion cost differs from the current-period rate.
Neither method is selected merely to obtain a preferred closing inventory. IAS 2.23–27 requires
specific identification where applicable and consistent FIFO or weighted-average treatment
for the relevant interchangeable inventories; the equivalent-unit calculations are the local
implementation of those cost policies (European Commission, 2023a, IAS 2.10–16, 23–27, 34).
For several stages, transfer the preceding stage’s reconciled cost into the next stage as a separate
cost component. Record when that component enters the process; it need not follow the
completion percentage of later materials or conversion. Each stage retains opening units and
costs, transfers in, completed transfers out, closing units, progress by component, inspection
points, losses and recoveries. An impossible completion fraction, unexplained physical loss,
missing opening cost or inconsistent transfer amount blocks the cost allocation rather than
becoming an unexplained unit-cost adjustment.
Abnormal loss cannot become a higher cost of the good units
Normal process loss and abnormal waste need different treatment. IAS 2.16(a) excludes abnormal
wasted materials, labour and other production costs from inventory. Its normal-capacity rule
also prevents idle fixed overhead from being hidden in a higher unit cost (European Commission,
2023a, IAS 2.12–16, 21). The normal-loss estimate must therefore be supported before observing
the actual loss, with its inspection stage and recoveries specified.
In a separate fully processed batch, 100 input units cost 1000. Assume normal yield is 95
units, all lost units have consumed the same full effort at inspection, there is no salvage, and
this cost pool contains no fixed overhead. The normal unit cost is 1000/95 = 200/19. If
actual good output is 90, five units of loss are normal and five abnormal. Good inventory
receives 90 × 200/19 = 18000/19, while abnormal loss of 1000/19 is expensed. At cents these
are 947.37 and 52.63, which reconcile to 1000. Allocating all 1000 to the ninety good units
would conceal the abnormal loss. Earlier inspection, partial conversion, recoverable scrap or a
different supported normal yield changes the calculation; those facts cannot be supplied by the
final good-unit count.

A pension surplus is useful only to the extent it is available
The preceding pension examples supplied a recoverable economic benefit. IFRIC 14 specifies
how the plan’s terms, statutory requirements and funding basis constrain that benefit. Separate
an unconditional right to a refund from the ability to reduce future contributions, and separate
funding for past service from funding for future service.
