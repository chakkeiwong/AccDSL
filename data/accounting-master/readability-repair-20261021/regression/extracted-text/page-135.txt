Selling all 60 good units in the low-output arm provides a second check. Production and its
payments are unchanged. Revenue becomes 600, sold-stock expense 348, profit 150 and cash
1210; inventory becomes zero. The additional 200 cash receipt releases inventory cost 116 and
increases profit by 84. Total pretax net assets become 1750. This is why a forecast needs
both production and sell-through, rather than an inventory margin inferred from total factory
spending.
Balanced books can still defer the wrong costs
If low output absorbed all fixed overhead using 180/60 = 3 per unit, unit cost would be 7.
Unsold stock would be 140 and profit 90, each 24 above the admitted result; cash would remain
1010. The books would balance, but they would postpone idle-capacity expense in the remaining
goods. At high output, applying 1.8 without a cap would allocate 270 of fixed cost when only
180 was incurred. Crediting a variance of 90 to balance that over-allocation would leave stock
174 and profit 564, both overstated by 18. A balanced variance cannot create an incurred cost.
Capitalizing abnormal loss 12 and unrelated period costs 18 into the 60-unit batch would defer
30 × 20/60 = 10 of excluded expense in unsold stock. This is a separate classification error from
the capacity error. Each resource therefore retains a purpose and identity, its membership in
the cost pool, and its cash or noncash origin. The state also retains capacity evidence, good
and lost units, batch cost and quantity, and the specific sale removals. A duplicate resource or
negative stock is invalid; a revised capacity estimate creates a new supported allocation decision
instead of overwriting an earlier result.
