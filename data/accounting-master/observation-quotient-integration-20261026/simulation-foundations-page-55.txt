Checking a supplied finite aggregation
A lending decision makes the joint-law requirement concrete. Suppose a company starts with
cash 10 and observes a green or red signal before deciding whether to lend. A good outcome
adds 2 to cash if it lends; a bad outcome subtracts 4. Waiting leaves cash unchanged. In this
hypothetical model, good and bad states each have probability one half, and the signal identifies
the state correctly with probability 9/10. The policy lends after green and waits after red. The
resulting cash movements +2, −4 and 0 have probabilities 9/20, 1/20 and 1/2. For example,
the profitable lending path needs both a good state and its green signal, so its probability is
(1/2)(9/10) = 9/20.
Two copies of the same initial state can be merged without changing those paths. But suppose
a second state reverses the signal’s association with the good outcome. Green and red still each
occur half the time, as do good and bad outcomes. The separate distributions therefore agree,
while the policy lends in different circumstances. Comparing the joint successor state and signal
detects the difference. A separate checked example keeps the successor state fixed and changes
dependence between observation and immediate cash movement; separate marginal checks miss
that change too.
The finite conformance helper checks this distinction for an explicitly supplied model and
proposed partition. At every date it compares permitted actions and current requested outputs
within each block. For every action and scenario it groups successor records by the complete
triple of successor block, observation and immediate output. In the probability case it sums
explicit rational masses and compares the exact totals; each supplied kernel must have total
mass one. In the unweighted case it compares sets of triples. Repeated representations of a
possibility never supply a probability.
Initial information needs the same care. The input specifies state and initial observation jointly.
Aggregation maps the state while retaining that observation and the current requested output.
Each later decision uses the retained history of observations, actions and outputs. A checked
first-decision example lends after one initial observation and waits after another, even though
their two states belong to the same block. Removing the observation would change the permitted
policy. A policy asking directly for the hidden state is outside this information interface.
The finite argument follows the preceding induction. At each successor block, the requested
current output is fixed by the block. Equality of the joint triples thus gives equal probabilities
for each extended observable history. Applying the same admissible policy and continuing
the induction preserves the whole path law. For an unweighted relation, compatible path
concatenation and union replace probability products and sums. Every quotient transition must
be possible from each member of its source block; otherwise an aggregated path might not lift
to a path in the original model.
An independently coded path enumerator checks twelve accepted finite cases, including every de-
terministic lend-or-wait choice after the two signals, duplicate successor states, initial-information
decisions and an unweighted model. Eight proposed merges fail with a specific unequal action,
output or joint-kernel component. Two deliberately incorrect representative merges also produce
different complete path laws. These checks support the supplied finite examples; they do not
establish that a company’s chosen state contains every economically relevant variable.
The helper refuses missing transitions, invalid probability mass, unrecognized information and
incomplete partitions. A comparison budget exhausted before all obligations are checked returns
an unknown result with the unfinished comparisons and no approved quotient. Saved results
bind the full model, partition, information, outputs and horizon; reuse rechecks the condition.
