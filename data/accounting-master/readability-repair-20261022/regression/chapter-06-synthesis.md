# Chapter 6: the mathematical meaning of a company history

Physical pages 84–96 supply the formal obligations left by the architecture.
The chapter starts with observations, their financial contexts and the declared
model before defining compatibility. This order matters: the same number in a
different perimeter or measurement basis is not another interchangeable solver
constraint. Empty, singleton and larger compatible sets have meanings relative
to that model; none alone establishes what actually happened at the company.

The creditor example gives unresolved histories a precise query consequence.
Every admitted history contains a payable of 100, but no creditor-amount row is
certain across the two histories. The answer family, row intersection and
Boolean certainty therefore answer different questions. The source discussion
retains Libkin's query, information-order and representation assumptions.
An inconclusive evaluator is also distinguished from evidence of opposing
histories. The subsequent nullspace argument explains one reason these families
exist, while the fixed/floating callback shows why even exact journal recovery
can leave economically consequential contract terms unknown.

The signed-book invariant then establishes what accounting can enforce exactly.
The induction is short and reconstructible. Its qualification is substantive:
balance does not validate recognition, ownership or treatment. Reporting uses
classified movements and economic state as well as balances, so a closing cash
amount cannot determine a cash-flow statement. The four-stage simulation maps
actions to economic events, accounting consequences and atomic state updates.
Its surrounding default and timing examples explain why those are separate
operations.
