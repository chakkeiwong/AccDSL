# Chapter 9 — operating-company methods

Version 3.47, physical pages 113–176, actually read in order in the bound
segments. This is the current author's diagnosis, not independent reader,
domain or implementability acceptance.

The chapter takes the prior history, state-transition and reference-method
design into an operating company. Its central argument is that a balanced
journal cannot identify what was promised, which costs survive, what rights
remain, what information was available, or which dated authority applies.
Those questions require retained economic histories and explicit evidence.
The argument is developed through consequences: an advance must not be used
twice; a refund liability and recovery asset settle separately; a replacement
removes an old component; an impairment floor redirects a loss; a correction
changes a comparative without changing its publication date.

The first sequence separates economic vocabulary, accounting packages and
company labels, then stages revenue, cost and obligation methods. The warranty
and modification cases distinguish earned service from cash and distinguish
prospective allocation from cumulative catch-up. PPE, production and pension
cases preserve the histories needed for subsequent events. Their alternative
balanced entries are useful because they isolate the actual error: a wrong
cost population, an omitted old asset, incorrect profit/OCI origin, or a
misstated cash category. The many numerical examples are lengthy, but their
prerequisites and later consequences remain locally accessible. I did not
confirm an additional local readability defect in this chapter.

The later cases make the dependencies more demanding. Opening WIP carries
both earlier cost and completed effort. Pension availability needs actual
refund and funding terms. Group impairment uses adjusted unit balances before
the wider test, and partial goodwill distinguishes the test population from
recognized assets. Tax follows the originating transaction and its own legal
conditions. The error-versus-estimate case connects these accounting results
to original evidence, discovery posting, restated comparisons and the two
publication/ingestion cutoffs. It gives the reader a concrete reason for the
time semantics introduced earlier.

Apple's asset and tax disclosures and Ping An's lease transition prevent the
synthetic cases from becoming implied reconstructions of real issuers. The
asset-book shortcut is rejected by a nonnegative retired-value constraint;
alternative local books need different company remainders and may produce
different future settlement paths. Conditional row invariance does not prove
that a full admissible Apple history exists. Tax residuals and lease adoption
differences likewise remain unexplained amounts, not invented entries.
Source precision, cash populations, maturity dates and narrative qualifiers
are preserved when they affect the interpretation.

The jurisdictional close adds an indispensable prerequisite to the numerical
methods. Historical technical support, operative authority, actual adoption
and entity facts remain separate. US current-source gaps are explicit; ASBE
lease adoption and later deferred-tax transition have distinct dates and
comparative consequences. The matched-case table is appropriate at this
point because its contrasts have already been explained. Moving it earlier
would turn conclusions into an unsupported checklist.

Equation exposition generally follows the numerical question with defined
quantities, declared timing and immediate interpretation. The capped
impairment equations retain their positive-weight domain, and the zero-weight
reversal remains unresolved. Billing and comparative identities are explained
by substitution rather than treated as evidence that underlying facts exist.
The chapter's removal would therefore lose substantive operating mechanisms;
swapping it with financing/group extensions would also weaken the reader's
understanding of the operating state that funding and ownership change.
