The following schedules make the distinction between performance and billing explicit. For
one performance obligation with price 100, cumulative revenue 60, unconditional bills 50 and
collection 30, AR=20, CA=10, CL=0 and cash=30. Revenue 60 is represented by those three
assets. Billing before performance changes intermediate balances but not the final result. For
bills 80 instead, AR=50, CA=0, CL=20; net assets remain 60. Cumulative performed-service
cost 40 then 70 posts 40 then 30, not 110. A downward revision follows the correction method
and keeps the old result tied to its original view.
For this simple contract, let R be cumulative recognised revenue, B unconditional billings and C
collections, with 0 ≤C ≤B. The unpaid bills determine accounts receivable (AR); performance
in excess of billing is a contract asset (CA), while billing in excess of performance is a contract
liability (CL). After each event,
AR = B −C,
CA = max(R −B, 0),
CL = max(B −R, 0),
cash received = C.
This recurrence applies per contract, with no impairment, variable consideration or unbilled
prepayment; it is not permission to offset unrelated contracts. For billing 80 before delivery,
debit AR 80/credit CL 80. Recognizing service 60 later debits CL 60 and credits revenue
60. Collecting 30 debits cash 30/credits AR 30, leaving AR 50 and CL 20, without a second
receivable or an artificial contract asset. If service comes first, recognize CA 60/revenue 60,
then bill AR 80 against CA 60 and CL 20. Both orders end at the same state. Cash received
before an unconditional bill needs an explicit advance event that debits cash and credits CL
and records the advance applied to later billing; it cannot pass through collect(AR).
9.4.1
A payment before the bill must be applied only once
The preceding recurrence deliberately excludes an advance before billing. Suppose a customer
pays 30 towards a service priced at 100. No service has yet been performed and no payment
was already due. The company now has cash 30 and an obligation to provide the service, so
it debits cash and credits a contract liability. Crediting revenue would balance the entry but
recognise performance that has not occurred. Paragraphs 105–108 of the retained AASB 15
distinguish this payment from an unconditional receivable (Australian Accounting Standards
Board, 2022e).
Now let the company earn 60 before issuing a bill. Recognition releases the liability of 30
and creates a contract asset of 30, with a credit to revenue of 60. The later invoice shows
a gross amount of 80 and applies the customer’s advance of 30, leaving a new unconditional
right to 50. Its entry debits the receivable by 50, credits the contract asset by 30 and credits a
contract liability by 20. Creating a receivable of 80 would count the prepaid 30 again. When
the customer pays another 20, cash is 50, the receivable is 30 and the contract liability is 20.
Their net contribution to assets is the earned 60.
