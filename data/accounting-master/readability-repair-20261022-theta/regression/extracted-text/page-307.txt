12.17
A payment distribution still leaves compensation to choose
A justified distribution answers how much the insurer might pay. It does not determine how much
compensation the insurer requires for bearing that uncertainty. Two insurers can agree on every
possible payment and its probability while choosing different compensation. The distinction
also matters within one insurer: a payment quantile, an average of expensive outcomes and a
charge for future capital answer different questions. England, Verrall and Wüthrich compare
these choices in their retained accepted manuscript, keeping the risk measure and its tolerance
level separate from the underlying reserve distribution (England et al., 2019, Sections 5–6).
Consider an authored finite example in which the discounted remaining payment is 0, 4 or 10,
with probabilities 1/2, 1/4 and 1/4. All amounts use one currency and are present values at
today’s valuation date. These probabilities define the example, rather than being estimated from
the small claims triangle. The mean payment is 0/2+ 4/4 + 10/4 = 7/2. A method that returns
a total risk measure of 31/4 would therefore assign an additional amount of 31/4 −7/2 = 17/4.
The choice of the total measure still needs explanation. For this finite specification, write X for
the discounted payment and m = E[X] for its mean. If R(X) is the selected total risk measure,
the candidate compensation is the relative amount
AR = R(X) −m.
The subtraction separates expected payment from the additional allowance. It does not select
R, establish the probabilities, or admit this amount as an insurer’s accounting risk adjustment.
The entity’s method and non-financial-risk perimeter must support that further use.
12.17.1
A tail average must retain the probability at its boundary
Suppose the insurer examines the upper forty percent of the example’s payment distribution.
The largest payment, 10, occupies only one quarter of all outcomes. Completing the upper forty
percent requires another 3/20 of probability from the payment of 4. Discarding every outcome
equal to the cutoff would average only the payments of 10; keeping all such outcomes would
average half the distribution. Both would answer a different question.
To specify the cutoff precisely, let F(x) = P(X ≤x) be the cumulative distribution function.
We use its left quantile at level α,
Qα = inf{x : F(x) ≥α},
0 < α ≤1.
At α = 3/5, the cumulative probability is 1/2 at zero and 3/4 at four, so Q3/5 = 4. This
quantile is one possible total risk measure. Its level is an explicit preference or measurement
input, not a number deduced from the three payments.
