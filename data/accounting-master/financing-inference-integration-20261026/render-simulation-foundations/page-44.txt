5.13
Credit supply and rationing
A fair price does not imply an unlimited quantity is available. Lenders can face capital, liquidity,
concentration or mandate constraints. A borrower may be unable to issue enough securities at
any admissible terms. A model that solves only lender break-even can miss rationing.
Bank stress and balance-sheet models such as Halaj (2016); Behn et al. (2019) provide examples
where financing and adjustment depend on constraints. Their institutional settings should
not be transferred mechanically to a corporate issuer. The general lesson is that a financing
transaction needs a counterparty and feasible quantity, not only an interest rate.
An offer curve can therefore specify price, maximum quantity, conditions and expiry. A policy
chooses among feasible offers or records no acceptable offer. If the curve is uncertain, results
remain conditional on alternative credit-supply assumptions. A scenario can deliberately freeze
refinancing, but that restriction should be visible as a stress premise.
5.14
What dynamic financing models establish
For longer-lived debt, lender receipts depend on future issuance, investment, priority changes
and default choices. A candidate policy π produces a price qπ. If management optimizes while
holding an incompatible price fixed, the resulting policy may not be the one lenders priced. A
simultaneous solution must make the chosen policy optimal under its price and make the price
value that policy’s promised and default-state payments. A constrained contract-price input is
a useful alternative for mechanical stress testing, but its interpretation is conditional on that
price and availability.
Benzoni and coauthors make this distinction explicit in a dynamic model with fixed issuance
costs (Benzoni et al., 2023, Section 2, Propositions 1–2, Appendix A). Their debt quantity is face
value, its price is per unit of face, and shareholders choose default, issuance or inaction. Value
matching and smooth pasting generate a candidate barrier policy; off-policy deviations and a
supermartingale condition are required for equilibrium verification. The retained model assumes
zero default recovery and risk-neutral agents. It therefore illustrates the policy-price consistency
problem, but is not the positive-recovery solution required by the preceding example.
The alternative managerial model of Lambrecht and Myers places investment, borrowing, payout
and rents in a dated sources-and-uses constraint and imposes a no-Ponzi restriction (Lambrecht
and Myers, 2017, Sections 2–4). Its safe-debt solutions depend strongly on managerial preferences
and governance. In its exponential-utility example, the liquidation covenant protects lenders
under the stated diffusion dynamics. A jump across the boundary, delayed liquidation or costly
asset disposal would require a different recovery analysis. A closed-form policy derived under
those restrictions is a competitor to consider, not an identified default policy for every issuer.
