3.12
Learning can change the appropriate compression
Two histories may have equal current cash and equal next-period payments, yet differ in the
chance of a revealing report. If the report affects a later refinancing choice, their future decision
values can differ. A merge based only on current accounting totals discards this difference.
Suppose two contract states both pay 5 next year. In one state, a scheduled certificate will
reveal a restrictive covenant before the next refinancing date; in the other, no such information
arrives. If management or lenders condition their offers on that certificate, equal immediate
cash does not imply equal future financing. The observation process belongs in the transition
model used for compression.
This is why exact aggregation later requires equality of the joint law of successor state, observa-
tions and requested outputs for each allowed action. Preserving a marginal cash distribution is
too weak. A graph may share current arithmetic while retaining separate information states for
future decisions.

Shapiro defines rectangularity through equality between static worst-case evaluation and its
nested conditional counterpart (Shapiro, 2016, Definition 2.1, Theorem 2.1 and proof, Section 5).
His finite construction pastes conditional distributions across histories; it can enlarge the
family. A dynamic program for that enlarged family solves a changed robustness question. Its
convenience cannot justify claiming that it solved the original persistent-type problem.
Terminology also requires care. Pichler and Shapiro call a product family a rectangular setting,
but explicitly show that its original worst-case functional and its composite conditional functional
can differ (Pichler and Shapiro, 2021, Section 4.2, Example 4.7). Computing a conditional
law-based risk measure can also differ from conditioning an unconditional ambiguity set. Take
six equiprobable states and an observed three-state group with losses 0, 1, 2. Conditional
expected shortfall at level 1/2 within that group is 5/3. The unconditional six-state risk
envelope permits probabilities at most 1/3 per state. It can assign 1/6 to the loss-2 state,
zero to the other two group members, and 5/18 to each state outside the group. This is an
admissible probability law; after conditioning on the group it assigns probability one to loss
2. The conditional worst case over that envelope is therefore 2. The difference is between
two precise conditional risk definitions, not an arithmetic error (Pichler and Shapiro, 2021,
Example 3.2 and equations (3.1)–(3.5)).
