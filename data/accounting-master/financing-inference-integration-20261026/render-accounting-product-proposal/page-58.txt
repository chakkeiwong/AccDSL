An existing holder with ni shares has ownership fraction ni/N before and ni/(N + n) after
the issue, assuming that holder does not subscribe. This is percentage dilution. Its economic
consequence depends on the price received and the use of the proceeds.
For example, let N = 100, pre-issue equity value be 1,000, and issue 20 shares at 10. If the
cash adds 200 of value and there are no costs or other effects, post-issue value is 1,200 and each
share remains worth 10. The old holders’ percentage falls from 100 per cent to 100/120, but
their combined value remains 1,000. Percentage dilution is not necessarily a wealth loss.
If the same 20 shares are issued at 8 while pre-issue assets remain worth 1,000, post-issue value
is 1,160 and each share is worth 1, 160/120. Old holders then own 100(1, 160/120) = 966.666 . . ..
The transfer arises from the discounted subscription price under these assumptions. A model
that equates all share-count dilution with value destruction misses the first case; one that
ignores issue price misses the second.
6.3
Raising a target amount after a price shock
Suppose net funding need is K and a fixed cash fee is f. At a supplied price ps, required shares
are (K + f)/ps. A price decline therefore increases the share count required to raise the same
cash. If authorized issuance or investor demand limits n, the funding target may be infeasible.
Take K = 90, f = 10 and an initial subscription price 10. The company needs ten shares.
After a price shock to 5 it needs twenty.
If only fifteen shares may be issued under the
assumed authorization, maximum net proceeds become 15(5) −10 = 65, leaving a shortfall of
25. Increasing book equity by 90 would conceal both the share constraint and the cash shortfall.
The fee may instead be proportional, for example f = λG. Net proceeds are G(1 −λ) and
required gross proceeds are K/(1 −λ) for 0 ≤λ < 1. A fixed plus proportional fee gives
G = (K + f0)/(1 −λ). These equations specify different cost structures. The choice should
come from a declared offer or empirical assumption, not a silent generic parameter.
6.4
Pricing is not supplied by an accounting identity
The equation G = nps is an identity once shares and price are specified. It does not determine
investor willingness to pay. A subscription price can be an external offer, an assumed discount
to an observed market price, or the result of a valuation and demand model. Each supports a
different claim.
A public market quote can be stale, illiquid or refer to a different class. A large issue can affect
price. A distressed company may face a discontinuous demand schedule or no feasible offer. A
model that uses yesterday’s price for unlimited new shares assumes unlimited demand at that
price. That may be useful in a deliberately simplified scenario, but should not be presented as
a financing solution established by the statements.
The same caution applies to an endogenous equity value function.
It may value existing
shareholders’ future payouts under a particular policy and financing friction. Converting that
value into a new issue price requires assumptions about how new investors share those payouts
and how issuance changes the state. A scalar cost function η(e) for external equity is not a
complete security issuance model.
