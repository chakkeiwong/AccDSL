3.16
A correction can restore an excluded history
Changing the particle representation preserves the likelihood being computed. Correcting
a historical disclosure can change that likelihood itself.
Suppose two histories have prior
probabilities (1/2, 1/2). An old hard observation has likelihoods (1, 0), producing posterior
(1, 0). Its replacement has likelihoods (1/4, 3/4). Recomputing from the prior gives
πreplacement = (1/4, 3/4).
(3.16)
Multiplying the restricted old posterior by the replacement likelihood and normalizing instead
gives (1, 0). The operation conjoins the old observation with the new one. It cannot recover the
history the old assertion excluded. The new-to-old likelihood ratio is also undefined on that
restored history, where the denominator was zero.
A replacement therefore requires replay from a sufficiently informative preserved checkpoint, or
a separately justified bridge with the required support. The inference request must identify the
evidence revision and which assertion is replaced. Merely attaching a corrected document to an
existing particle population does not establish the corrected posterior. This is the probabilistic
counterpart of retaining alternative histories that become admissible after a source correction.
The source’s quantitative guarantees require additional premises. Its Appendix B uses uniform
transition and observation-density ratio bounds and posterior regularity conditions, and its
refreshed-particle idealization is stronger than a finite PMCMC move (Chopin et al., 2013,
Proposition 2 and Appendix B). A permanent deterministic type, a hard zero-likelihood
accounting observation or an unidentified parameter direction can violate the intended transfer
assumptions. Exact finite target calculations establish the distinctions above; they do not
establish company-model mixing, scaling, likelihood identification or forecast accuracy.
3.17
A proposed information service
AccDSL should represent evidence views and actor information views separately. An evidence
view selects actual assertions by availability, revision and scope.
An actor view specifies
observable state variables, private contractual facts and signal channels at each decision date.
Policies receive only that view and permitted memory.
Forward execution first applies events available at the current boundary, constructs observations,
updates the relevant information state, and then permits decisions according to the stated
timing. Alternative orderings are possible, but they must be explicit and tested. Moving a
decision across an observation boundary changes the economic problem.
The service should refuse ambiguous timing where it affects the answer. It can still return a
conditional comparison of both orderings. Such a comparison is preferable to silently selecting
whichever ordering makes the simulation easier. Exact timestamps, time zones and publication
lags matter when events occur on the same calendar date.
3.18
Tests that discriminate information errors
A useful test duplicates a world, changes an unobserved future shock and confirms that the
current action remains unchanged. Another changes a privately known contract term and
confirms that an informed manager’s policy may change while a public analyst’s evidence view
does not. A third inserts a later restatement and checks that an earlier frozen forecast remains
reproducible.
