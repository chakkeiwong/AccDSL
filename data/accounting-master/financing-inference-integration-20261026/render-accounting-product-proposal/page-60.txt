6.8
Repurchases and treasury shares
A repurchase uses cash and changes outstanding shares or treasury holdings according to the
legal and accounting treatment. A net increase in equity during a period can conceal both gross
issuance and gross repurchase. Their timing and prices matter for liquidity and ownership.
Suppose 20 shares are issued at 10 and ten are repurchased at 12. Net cash inflow before costs
is 80, while outstanding shares increase by ten if repurchased shares cease to be outstanding.
Reconstructing the period as a simple issue of ten shares for 80 uses an implied price of 8
and loses the actual transactions. A future policy inferred from that synthetic price can be
misleading.
Repurchase authorization is not settlement. A board may authorize up to an amount while
management executes only part. A scenario should not consume cash for the entire authorization
unless the policy calls for it and execution is feasible. Conversely, an already binding purchase
commitment may create obligations before the cash settlement date.
6.9
Dividends require both claims and capacity
A dividend policy can specify a target payout, but payment depends on declaration, entitlement
dates, cash, legal restrictions, covenants and class rights. Retained earnings are not a cash
account. A positive accumulated balance does not establish that funds are available in the
paying entity.
A declared dividend can create a payable before payment. The event sequence must avoid
expensing it as an operating cost in an illustrative ordinary distribution model, while still
recognizing the liability and equity movement under the selected basis. Payment reduces cash
and the payable. The exact legal and accounting treatment requires its admitted rules.
A cash-constrained policy may reduce or suspend a prospective dividend. It cannot necessarily
cancel an already enforceable obligation. Distinguishing intended, declared and paid distributions
is therefore important in stress scenarios. A single annual dividend line cannot capture all of
these states.
6.10
Ownership and control are related but distinct
Economic ownership percentages do not always determine control. Voting rights, contractual
arrangements, dispersed holdings and substantive options can matter. A scenario that changes
ownership may alter the consolidation perimeter, but the threshold and assessment are not
supplied by a generic percentage rule.
The security register should record holders and rights, while a separate control assessment uses
the applicable facts and accounting basis. When control changes, group reporting can change
even without a large contemporaneous cash flow. Conversely, a minority issue can raise cash
without changing control. The two calculations should communicate without being collapsed.
Historical disclosures may report total shares but not every beneficial holder. A model can
represent ownership categories and unknown allocations where sufficient for the query. It must
not invent holder identities or treat an unknown allocation as evenly spread. Concentration can
matter for approval, takeover and financing decisions.
