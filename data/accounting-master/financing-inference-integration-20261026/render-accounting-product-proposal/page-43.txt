Chapter 4
Scenarios, interventions, and feasible
decisions
A scenario is a specified change to a model, not merely a new column of forecast numbers. It
must say what changes, what remains structurally linked, what actors observe and how they
may respond. A rate increase imposed on a contract schedule differs from conditioning an
empirical model on periods when rates were high. A revenue decline with unchanged prices
differs from one caused by price reductions. These distinctions determine the cash flows and
the meaning of the result.
4.1
Describe the experiment before calculating it
A scenario request should identify the forecast origin, horizon, evidence view, shock variables,
shock timing, transition rules, management policy and requested outputs. It should also specify
whether the shock is a deterministic path, a random process or an ambiguity set. A phrase
such as “rates up 200 basis points” leaves reset dates, currency curves, spread behavior and
duration unresolved.
For a deterministic scenario s, let ut(s) be the imposed external path. The company evolves as
Xt+1 = Tt(Xt, θ, at, ut+1(s)),
at = πt(It).
(4.1)
The transition includes contractual events and operational behavior. The policy uses information
available at its decision time. A statement projection is then a reporting map of the resulting
events and states. Directly shocking a reported total can be a useful reduced-form approximation,
but it requires an explanation of how the corresponding events and balances are reconstructed.
A scenario can also specify a set S of paths. The outcome family becomes
Y = {F(h, s, π) : h ∈H, s ∈S, π ∈Π}.
(4.2)
This expression is not automatically a Cartesian product of arbitrary histories, shocks and
policies. Feasibility may couple them. A lender’s offer can depend on the company’s contract
state; a commodity shock can affect both sales and collateral; a policy may be admissible only
for certain information states. These dependencies must remain in the defining relation.
