7.9
A two-entity liquidity example
Parent P has cash 5 and external debt due 20. Subsidiary S has cash 30 and no current external
debt. Consolidated cash is 35, apparently enough for the maturity. Suppose only 10 can legally
and operationally transfer from S before the due date under the declared facts. P can then
assemble 15 and has a shortfall of 5.
If a permitted intercompany loan of 15 becomes available in time, P can pay. If a dividend of
15 is declared but settles after maturity, it cannot fund that payment without a bridge. If the
subsidiary cash is restricted, even the first 10 may be unavailable. The scenario result depends
on transfer rights and timing, not just the consolidated total.
A correct consolidated statement after payment can coexist with an incorrect event sequence
if the model silently borrowed the cash before it was available. Tests must therefore examine
entity cash at event boundaries, not only the final group balance sheet.
7.10
Taxes and losses
Tax expense, cash tax and deferred tax are different quantities. A profit forecast cannot generally
multiply pretax income by one rate and use the result simultaneously for expense and immediate
payment. Loss carryforwards, payment lags, jurisdiction, interest limitations and temporary
differences can separate them.
A simple teaching model may assume immediate tax payment at rate τ on positive taxable
income, giving cash tax τ max(T, 0). That is a declared simplification. If losses can offset future
income, the state must retain the remaining loss balance and expiry or utilization conditions.
Forgetting that state can overstate future tax; treating all losses as immediately refundable can
overstate current cash.
Accounting recognition of deferred tax assets requires its own admitted rules and evidence.
Their carrying amount is not spendable cash. A stress scenario that turns a deferred tax asset
into a funding source without an actual refund or financing transaction is economically wrong.
7.11
Estimates, errors and changes of policy
An estimate revision changes the model’s measurement of an uncertain quantity. An error
correction revises a prior assertion or application. An accounting-policy change can alter
recognition or measurement according to the applicable rules. Their timing and comparative
effects differ.
The scenario engine should distinguish an economic shock that changes expected credit losses
from a correction showing that the old receivables data were wrong. Both can change reported
equity, but only one necessarily describes new economic information at the forecast date. The
evidence and accounting version relations should make the distinction explicit.
