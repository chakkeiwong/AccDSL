An added item layer begins at the rebalancing date; the earlier layer keeps its old reference.
The source’s example has 100 tonnes designated at forward price 80 and adds 10 tonnes
when the price is 90. In a zero-discount illustrative comparison at price 95, their changes
are 100(95 −80) + 10(95 −90) = 1550. Applying the old price to all 110 tonnes gives 1650,
inventing a gain of 100 on the added layer. Actual measurement must use the admitted risk,
dates, discounting and contractual terms.
Adding an instrument similarly starts its change at the new designation date without resetting
the old instrument. Decreasing designated instrument volume leaves any retained undesignated
derivative at fair value through profit or loss unless another qualifying designation applies. For
example, removing 10% of a homogeneous instrument from the designation leaves its total
carrying value unchanged; of a subsequent total fair-value gain 10, the undesignated 1 enters
profit and the designated 9 follows the continuing hedge’s effectiveness test. This split does not
assert that all 9 is effective. Decreasing item volume invokes partial-discontinuation rules for
the removed part. Whether its forecast cash flows remain expected determines the reserve’s
later release; removal from designation alone does not mean cancellation.
10.12.7
A new ratio does not restart cumulative measurement
The origin of an added layer matters again at every later measurement. Consider a continuing
hedge of purchases with an unchanged hedging instrument. The original purchase volume is
100 tonnes, designated at a forward price of 80 currency units per tonne. For this illustration,
assume zero discounting and one common price risk; actual valuations must support those
simplifications. When the price reaches 90, the cumulative loss on the hedged purchase is 1000.
A separately supplied cumulative derivative gain of 1200 gives reserve 1000 and cumulative
ineffectiveness income 200.
Suppose the documented economic relationship now requires ten more tonnes to be designated,
while the risk-management objective remains unchanged. Recognize the old designation’s
ineffectiveness first, then add those tonnes at their current price of 90. At a later price of 95,
the cumulative hedged-item loss is
100(95 −80) + 10(95 −90) = 1550.
If the cumulative derivative gain is then 1400, IFRS 9.6.5.11 requires a reserve of
min(1400, 1550) = 1400, leaving cumulative ineffectiveness zero. The derivative has gained
only 200 since the last observation, but reserve must increase by 400: debit derivative 200,
debit ineffectiveness expense 200 and credit reserve 400. The earlier income of 200 reverses
(European Commission, 2023a, IFRS 9, paragraphs 6.5.11(a)–(c), B6.5.8 and B6.5.17).
Restarting the lower-of calculation at the ratio change gives a different, incorrect answer. It
adds the smaller of the new derivative gain 200 and new item loss 550 to the old reserve, leaving
reserve 1000 + 200 = 1200 and profit 200. That journal can balance, yet it fails the cumulative
rule. Rebalancing continues the relationship; it does not turn successive measurement intervals
into separate hedges.
