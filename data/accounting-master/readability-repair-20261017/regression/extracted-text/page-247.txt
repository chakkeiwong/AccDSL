Rounding the resulting score is a separate operation. In the background to its March 2026
proposal, the Federal Reserve describes scores as rounded to the nearest basis point: fractions
below one-half round down and fractions at or above one-half round up (Board of Governors of
the Federal Reserve System, 2026a, p. 14909, n. 9). The earlier description appears in the 2015
final rule’s preamble, within its account of the preceding proposal (Board of Governors of the
Federal Reserve System, 2015, p. 49084, n. 22). These passages provide an official explanation of
the convention; they do not complete the amendment history needed to establish its applicability
on a chosen date. The 2026 proposal also contains a separate ceiling formula for Method 2
surcharge bands and changes to indicator averaging and denominator exchange rates. Those
proposed changes cannot be substituted for the rule applicable to the bank and date being
reconstructed (Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, 2026a, pp. 14917–14922).
The report’s quarter-end date differs from its submission deadline and public availability;
amendments, later resubmissions and confidentiality can change what a historical reader could
know. The retained instruction file itself combines a September 2021 cover with a January 2026
reporting-scope page. Applying it consequently requires the relevant page and reporting vintage,
alongside the applicable rule and institution facts. These sources explain the input requirements;
they do not yet supply a verified bank-specific score or a complete reporting-rule history (Board
of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, 2026d, cover and GEN-1–5; physical pp. 1, 5–9).
11.1.5
Debt measurement, payment rights and succession
The payout rules constrain cash leaving the bank. Debt contracts determine a different set of
claims on the group: who owes a payment, when it falls due, and what a holder can require
after a breach. Debt amounts therefore need their own measurement and contractual histories.
Note 20 puts US$253,039m of long-term-debt carrying value under Parent company for 2025,
while Note 33’s parent-only balance sheet shows US$254,633m. The US$1,594m difference
equals Note 20’s separately displayed junior-subordinated-debt subtotal. The same comparison
gives US$1,603m in 2024. This exact two-year arithmetic match does not establish how that row
relates to the parent-only balance, or which legal entity owes each instrument. Its accounting
and contractual relation still needs source support.
Timing requires a different measure. Note 20 places US$14,298m of parent carrying value in
its under-one-year bucket; Note 33 reports US$14.3bn of parent long-term debt contractually
maturing in 2026. They align at the footnote’s displayed precision, but carrying value includes
premiums, discounts, costs and valuation adjustments rather than simply principal payable.
For the separately reported consolidated zero-coupon population, Note 20 gives US$18.1bn of
carrying value against US$57.6bn of aggregate principal at the notes’ respective maturities. The
firm’s US$435,206m consolidated long-term-debt total and US$47.6bn of subsidiary debt and
structured notes guaranteed by the parent are also separate from the parent’s own debt balance;
the latter guarantee amount supplies neither a liability valuation nor a payment forecast. A
dated parent debt-service path still needs instrument terms, cash dates and permissible funding
transfers (JPMorgan Chase & Co., 2026, pp. 281–282, 310–311).
