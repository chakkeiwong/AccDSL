Expected credit loss (ECL) measures the discounted expected cash shortfall. A significant
increase in credit risk (SICR) changes the general method’s default horizon. Purchased or
originated credit-impaired (POCI) assets follow a separate method. For the general approach,
twelve-month ECL considers lifetime shortfalls arising from possible defaults during the next
twelve months; it is not just unpaid cash in those twelve months. SICR uses the change
in lifetime default risk since initial recognition, reasonable forward-looking information and
the applicable rebuttable presumptions. Do not install a two-times-PD or 30-day rule as an
unconditional universal trigger. Lifetime ECL follows a supported SICR finding; cure needs
a supported reversal of that finding. Subsequently credit-impaired assets use original EIR on
amortised cost for interest in subsequent periods; POCI has a separate credit-adjusted-rate and
cumulative-change method.
The 2017 ASBE 22 text makes the two horizons explicit. Article 47 defines credit loss as the
present value of all contractual cash flows less expected receipts; a late receipt can therefore
create a loss even when its nominal amount is paid in full. Article 48 limits twelve-month
ECL by the date of the possible default event, while retaining the resulting cash shortfalls
over the asset’s life. Article 52 assesses changes in default risk, rather than changes in the
allowance, and Article 58 requires probability weighting, time value and reasonable, supportable
information (Ministry of Finance of the People’s Republic of China, 2017, Articles 47–48, 52,
58). These provisions support the following dated comparison. Article 84 states 1 January
2018, but applying the standard to a particular company also requires its issuance schedule,
subsequent amendments and adoption facts.
The general measurement interface requires scenario_cashflows, law_ref, information_view,
horizon_admission, credit_assessment and discount_rule. An unweighted history set can
produce conditional losses or loss bounds, but cannot produce an unbiased probability-weighted
ECL by silently using equal weights. Return measurement_unresolved for the ECL claim.
Trade receivables and certain other assets use the separately applicable simplified approach; the
general loan staging rule must not overwrite AASB 9.5.5.15–16.
11.6
A commitment carries its credit history into the loan
A promise to lend can create a credit-loss provision before it creates a loan or consumes cash.
Once the customer draws, the uncertainty about whether funding will occur has been resolved,
but the customer’s earlier credit history has not disappeared. The distinction connects the
commitment populations reported above to the funded-loan method that follows. AASB 9.5.5.6
dates the initial impairment comparison from the irrevocable commitment; B5.5.47 treats the
resulting financial asset as a continuation of that commitment (Australian Accounting Standards
Board, 2022h).
Consider a synthetic bank with cash 200, deposits 180 and equity 20. It grants an irrevocable
commitment allowing one immediate draw of 100 or lapse. The contract is fixed rather than
revolving, cannot be net settled, is not designated at fair value through profit or loss, and does
not promise a below-market loan. No fee or separate noncontingent commitment liability is
assumed. The bank will hold the resulting loan to collect principal and interest. Its admitted
initial fair value is 100, equal to its cash price, and its payment of 121 after two years gives
an effective interest rate of 10%. These are explicit measurement and classification facts. The
credit probabilities below do not themselves establish market value; a different initial value or a
below-market commitment would require the separate analysis under AASB 9.2.3, 4.2.1(d) and
5.1.1–5.1.1A.
