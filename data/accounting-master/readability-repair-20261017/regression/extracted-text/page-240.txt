One invoice may correspond to several ledger lines. An explicit allocation of 40 and 60 can
exhaust a receivable of 100; reusing the 40 creates an invalid allocation of 140. Each source
amount is consumed at most once in a given consolidation version. A receivable of 100 against
a payable of 90 leaves an unresolved difference of 10 until a settlement, cutoff correction or
other supported adjustment explains it. Equal labels, a balancing entry or a stale source
revision cannot supply that explanation. Unknown matches remain visible and prevent a
complete-consolidation result for that view. These identity rules are implementation choices for
preserving the source meaning; they are not a claim that IFRS prescribes a database key.
Now follow one correctly matched inventory sale. The wholly owned subsidiary begins with cash
and capital of 50. The parent holds cash 90, inventory 60 and the investment 50, with equity
200. All amounts use one currency; taxes, impairment and other transactions are absent by
assumption. The parent sells the inventory to the subsidiary for 100 on credit. The subsidiary
then sells 40 percent of those goods to an external customer for cash 60, recording cost 40 and
retaining inventory 60. The parent’s profit is 40 and the subsidiary’s profit 20, but the group
has earned only 36: external revenue 60 less the original cost 24 of the goods sold.
Eliminate the investment 50 against subsidiary opening equity 50 and the payable 100 against
receivable 100. Of the internal markup 40, the fraction still in inventory is 60 percent. The
performance adjustment therefore debits internal revenue 100 and credits cost of sales 76 and
inventory 24. Combined cost of sales 100 becomes 24, inventory 60 becomes 36, and profit
60 becomes 36. Closing group cash 200 plus inventory 36 equals opening equity 200 plus
profit 36. The same result follows directly from the external history: opening group cash 140
plus customer receipt 60, with inventory cost 60 less 24. The adjustment retains the lot and
external-sale references needed to release the remaining markup when those goods leave the
group. Actual tax bases, upstream NCI allocation and evidence that an intragroup loss reflects
impairment require their own consequences under IFRS 10.B86(c). The earlier foreign-loan case
also remains: eliminating a monetary balance does not automatically erase its exchange gain
(European Commission, 2023a, IFRS 10.B86(c); IAS 21.45).
10.23
Restating purchasing power before translating currency
An inventory cost and a bank balance of the same nominal amount do not retain the same
purchasing power. IAS 29 addresses this problem when the functional currency belongs to
a hyperinflationary economy. Its paragraph 3 requires judgment across economic indicators;
three-year inflation approaching or exceeding 100 percent is one indicator, not an automatic test.
The selected assessment, effective period and general price index must therefore accompany the
entity book (European Commission, 2023a, IAS 29.1–4, 8–19, 24–28, 37–39).
For historical-cost nonmonetary amounts, retain each acquisition or measurement date and
restate from that date’s index to the closing index. Restate income and expense from recognition
dates, and contributed equity from the relevant contribution dates. Monetary balances remain at
their closing nominal amounts; contractually index-linked amounts first follow their contractual
adjustment. Amounts already measured at closing current values are not indexed again. After
restatement, apply the relevant recoverability or net-realizable-value limit. Missing dates or
indices require the specific permitted estimation procedure and its support; an unexplained
common multiplier is insufficient.
