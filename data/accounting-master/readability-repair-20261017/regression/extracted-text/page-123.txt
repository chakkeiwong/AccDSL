9.4.5
The same cash can accompany different revenue
Suppose an entity receives 120 from a customer and sends 100 to a supplier. It keeps 20. Those
cash movements cannot tell us whether it sold the supplier’s good or arranged a sale by that
supplier. Under B34–B38, the decision starts with the specified good or service and whether
the entity controls it before transfer to the customer. Responsibility for delivery, inventory risk
and price discretion inform that decision; none is a substitute for control, and momentary legal
title does not by itself establish it (Australian Accounting Standards Board, 2022e).
In the principal case, the entity controls the identified good before customer transfer, can direct
its use and obtain its benefits, and is responsible for delivering it. Acquisition on credit debits
inventory 100 and credits the supplier payable 100. Customer transfer and cash receipt debit
cash 120 and credit revenue 120; the inventory leaves through cost of sales 100. Settling the
supplier debits the payable and credits cash 100. From zero opening balances, the result is cash
and equity 20, revenue 120 and profit 20.
In the agent case, the supplier controls the good until transfer; the entity only arranges the
sale and earns a fixed commission of 20 when that service is completed. Assume the entity
controls the collected cash and owes the supplier 100 under its remittance agreement. Funds
held outside its balance sheet in a fiduciary capacity are outside this example. Receipt therefore
debits cash 120, credits the supplier payable 100 and credits commission revenue 20. Settlement
clears the payable against cash 100. The agent has no inventory or supplier cost of sales. Its
final cash, equity and profit are again 20, but revenue is 20.
Even the intermediate balance sheets agree: before remittance, both have cash 120, a payable
100 and equity 20. The different control facts explain the different revenue. The method must
retain those facts and classify each specified promise separately, since the same entity can act as
principal for one promise and agent for another. An unknown control finding leaves classification
unresolved; the receipt cannot decide it. Both cash legs remain in the ledger. Whether the
cash-flow statement presents receipts and remittances gross or net requires its own AASB 107
conditions, which do not follow merely from the revenue classification. These examples establish
the cash change and pretax profit contribution while preserving that separate presentation
question.
The customer cases separate earned revenue from billing and cash. The cost side requires a
second history: which goods and asset components remain, what they cost, and which losses or
revaluations they have already absorbed. Ordinary cost and replacement cases establish that
history before production and changed obligations make it more complex.
9.4.6
Inventory and assets retain their cost histories
Inventory uses identified cost for non-interchangeable items or the admitted cost formula for
interchangeable items. AASB 102.9, 23, 25 and 28–34 support lower of cost/net realizable
value, relevant cost formulas and write-down/reversal conditions. A method never chooses LIFO

3, followed by sale of 12 units. FIFO gives cost 26 and stock 8 units/cost 24. Weighted average
