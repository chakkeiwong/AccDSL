To see the opening-equity effect, suppose the old carrying liabilities are 105 for A and 115 for B.
Assets of 500 remain unchanged, and these are the only liabilities. Before transition, equity is
500 −105 −115 = 280. Afterwards, it is 500 −110 −120 = 270. Each group’s liability increases
by 5, so total opening equity falls by 10. Paragraph C5 replaces the retrospective measurement
requirement in C4(a); the removal of incompatible old balances and the net adjustment to
equity in C4(b)–(c) still apply. The ordinary initial-recognition loss rule does not turn this
transition adjustment into current-period operating expense (Australian Accounting Standards
Board, 2022f, paragraphs C4–C5).
Illustrative component entries show the same reconciliation. For A, debit the old liability 105
and opening equity 5, then credit the FCF liability 90 and the CSM liability 20. For B, debit
the old liability 115 and opening equity 5, then credit the FCF liability 120. Record B’s loss
component of 20 within that 120 for its subsequent allocation; it produces no additional credit.
Neither entry changes cash. An actual transition would also need the acquisition, reinsurance,
tax and OCI effects excluded here.
The order of calculation matters as much as the signs. If an implementation first combined
the two already distinct groups, it would find total FCF of 210 and total fair value of 210.
Applying the sign rule to those totals would produce neither CSM nor a loss component and
would report a liability of 210. The correct group-by-group calculation gives 230. Pooling
would therefore understate liability and overstate equity by 20. The programme must retain
the admitted group boundary while calculating CSM and loss components, then aggregate the
resulting balances. Whether different grouping is itself permitted requires the separate grouping
rules; an aggregation shortcut cannot decide that accounting question.
The example supplies the arithmetic once the group’s two measurements and old balances are
known. A real transition disclosure may reveal only some of those quantities. The later Munich
Re case shows both what can be read from an insurer’s report and what still requires contract
and valuation records.
