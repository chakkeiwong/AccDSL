Acquisition debits the two asset costs by 80 and 20 and credits cash by 100. Paragraphs 43–44
require separate depreciation of significant components; the full year’s charge is 80/8+20/2 = 20.
Debit depreciation expense 20 and credit each component’s accumulated depreciation by 10. At
31 December 2024, cash is 100, net PPE is 80 and equity is 180 after the loss of 20. No revenue
or other operating activity is included in this asset-only example.
On 1 January 2025 the company replaces the old component. Its remaining carrying amount
is 20 −10 = 10. Debit accumulated depreciation 10 and disposal loss 10, and credit the old
component’s cost 20. The replacement costs 30, is paid in cash and is available immediately
for three years of even use, again with zero residual. Its entry debits new PPE 30 and credits
cash 30. Cash is now 70 and PPE is 70 + 30 = 100; equity is 170. Retaining the old component
would instead report PPE of 110 and miss the loss of 10, although both acquisition entries
would balance.
The requirement to remove the old part also applies when it was not depreciated separately.
Paragraph 70 permits replacement cost as an indication of the old part’s cost at acquisition if
its carrying amount cannot practicably be determined. That does not make today’s invoice the
old part’s remaining value. Acquisition-date cost and consumption would still need a supported
estimate. Ordinary servicing has a different consequence: paragraph 12 expenses it as incurred.
A separately assumed maintenance payment of 4 would debit maintenance expense and credit
cash by 4. Capitalising it would balance but overstate both PPE and profit by 4 under those
facts.
During 2025 the shell and new component each depreciate by 10. Immediately before sale on 31
December, gross active cost is 80 + 30 = 110, accumulated depreciation is 20 + 10 = 30, and net
PPE is 80. Cash remains 70 and equity is 150. The buyer then obtains control and pays 90, with
no selling costs or sale-and-leaseback arrangement. Remove gross cost 110 and accumulated
depreciation 30, debit cash 90, and credit disposal gain 10. Paragraphs 67–72 locate the disposal
at control transfer and measure the gain as net proceeds less carrying amount. The gain is not
ordinary revenue; routine sales of formerly rented assets have a separate rule at 68A.

The cash-flow statement separates the 2025 purchase of 30 from the sale proceeds of 90. Under
the retained AASB 107.16(a)–(b), both are investing flows. The indirect operating reconciliation
removes depreciation and the non-operating loss and gain from profit:
CFO2024 = −20 + 20 = 0,
CFO2025 = −20 + 20 + 10 −10 = 0.
Lifetime investing cash is −100 −30 + 90 = −40, equal to the equity reduction once no assets
remain. An ordinary servicing payment would instead be operating cash; paragraph 16 does
not permit investing classification merely because an expense relates to equipment (Australian
Accounting Standards Board, 2023a).
