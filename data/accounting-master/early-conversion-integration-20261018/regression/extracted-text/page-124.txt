9.4.7
Preparing equipment and selling its first samples
Preparing a machine can produce two things of value: equipment capable of operating as
intended, and goods that can already be sold.
Their costs and receipts answer different
accounting questions. Necessary delivery, installation and functional testing contribute to the
machine’s cost under AASB 116.16–17. Paragraph 20A requires the proceeds from selling
items produced during preparation, and those items’ costs, to enter profit under the applicable
standards. It measures the goods’ costs using AASB 102 (Australian Accounting Standards
Board, 2022b, 2021a). The samples’ margin therefore does not reduce the cost of the machine.
Consider a synthetic Australian manufacturer in calendar 2024, starting with cash and con-
tributed capital of 300. It pays 100 for equipment, 5 for its initial delivery, 12 for necessary
installation and 3 for an engineering service that tests and calibrates its physical performance.
Probable benefits and reliable cost measurement are admitted. These four amounts give PPE
cost of 120. The engineering service uses different resources from those recorded as sample
production below. Each receipt or resource has an identity and an assigned purpose; sharing a
resource would require an evidenced allocation, not two recognitions of its full cost.
The sole sample batch uses normal materials costing 4 and directly attributable contracted
conversion costing 2. The example has no other production overheads. Its goods cost is thus 6
under AASB 102.10–15. In a real factory, paragraph 13 requires fixed production overheads to
be allocated using normal capacity; low output does not justify loading every unused resource
onto a few units. Unknown overheads would leave the goods’ cost unresolved here. Separate
abnormal production waste of 1 is expensed under paragraph 16(a). Staff training of 3 and
advertising of 2 are also period expenses, excluded from PPE by AASB 116.19. All these
amounts are paid in cash.
On 30 June, the manufacturer sells the entire batch for cash of 8. These are ordinary manufac-
tured goods, outside the inventory standard’s agricultural, financial-instrument and broker-trader
exceptions. Net realisable value is 8, with no further completion or selling costs, so cost 6 needs
no write-down. The approved, enforceable customer contract identifies the goods and price, has
commercial substance and probable collection. Delivery, acceptance, title and control transfer
together on that date. There are no remaining promises, returns or price concessions in this
case. These facts admit the revenue treatment under AASB 15.9 and 31–38; cash receipt alone
would not establish it (Australian Accounting Standards Board, 2022e).

expense 6 and credit cash 6. At sale, debit cash 8 and credit revenue 8, then debit cost of sales
6 and credit inventory 6. AASB 102.34 links the goods’ expense to recognition of the related
