12.15
A reserve estimate is not a future payment
The reserve of 148 leaves an important question unanswered: how much might the insurer
ultimately pay? Repeating an estimation procedure can show how its fitted answer varies.
Even if that answer were known exactly, however, the future claims would still vary. England’s
retained 2001 working addendum makes this distinction operational: first resample and refit
to obtain future conditional means, then simulate payments conditional on those means. The
inspected text is Actuarial Research Paper 138; its exact equivalence to the separately published
2002 journal addendum has not been established (England, 2001, Sections 2–3 and Appendix
A).
Consider a small probability example, separate from the triangle. Suppose a fitted future mean,
denoted by M, is either 2 or 8 with equal probability. These are authored possibilities, not
estimates from an insurer. If M = 2, let a payment Y be 0 or 4, equally likely; if M = 8, let it
be 4 or 12, equally likely. Within each fitted case, the payment has mean M and variance 2M.
Across cases, the fitted mean has expectation 5 and variance 9. The total variation in payments
includes both the variation left within a case and the variation between case means:
Var(Y ) = E[Var(Y | M)] + Var(E[Y | M]) = E[2M] + Var(M) = 10 + 9 = 19.
One can check the result directly: Y is 0, 4 or 12 with probabilities 1/4, 1/2 and 1/4. Its mean
is 5, and its variance is (25/4) + (1/2) + (49/4) = 19. Reporting the distribution of fitted means
alone would instead give variance 9 and no payment above 8. The full authored payment law
gives probability 1/4 to that event.
