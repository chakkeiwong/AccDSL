2.7.1
Cash paid and expense incurred answer different questions
The bond’s original liability proceeds were 90 and its original equity component was 10. Accrued
interest of 10 has already increased the opening liability to 100. Redemption on 2 January
therefore debits debt 100 and credits cash 100. It creates no new 2025 interest expense. Under
this company’s historical IAS 7 operating election, the payment is financing principal 90 and
operating interest 10. Interest paid must still be disclosed separately even when the associated
expense belongs to another period (European Commission, 2023a, IAS 7.31–33).
A component adapter must consequently preserve the original debt cash basis and the accu-
mulated liability. Carrying amount alone cannot reconstruct the cash-flow split. Nor can an
event labelled “accrue” simply be moved across a year boundary: the opening states whether
its expense has already been recorded. Here P’s cash falls to 400, the debt becomes zero and
original equity remains 10.
The return liability comes from a previous ten-unit sale. Each unit originally cost 12 and can be
refunded for 20. Expected returns were two units; estimated handling cost 3 and deterioration
1 per unit gave a recovery asset 2(12 −3 −1) = 16 and refund liability 2(20) = 40. Two units
actually return. Handling is 4 per unit, deterioration is 2 and the returned stock’s supplied net
realisable value is 20. All return rights are now closed.
The stock recovered is min{2(12 −2), 20} = 20. P pays refunds 40 and handling 8, releases the
refund liability 40 and recovery asset 16, and records inventory 20. Because the actual handling
is explicitly a selling expense, the remaining entries are a cost-of-sales credit of 4 and selling
expense 8. Their net expense is 4; there is no revenue adjustment when actual refunds equal the
liability. This separates the recovery estimate from the cash actually spent and avoids charging
handling twice (European Commission, 2023a, IFRS 15.B21–B27; IAS 2.16).
Tax timing supplies the next link. The original sale was already taxable, and its original
inventory cost was deducted. On return, tax deducts the refund 40, restores recovered inventory
20 to tax stock and deducts handling 8. Thus the return reduces taxable income by 28, compared
with book expense 4. The difference is 24. Opening deferred tax asset 10 and liability 4 reverse,
giving deferred tax expense 6. A zero-tax return example would miss this interaction.
2.7.2
The subsidiary’s inventory contains the parent’s margin
During the first half P also pays wages 10 and records depreciation 10. It then sells its other
inventory, which cost 60, to S for 140 on credit. P recognises revenue 140 and cost of sales
60; S records inventory and a payable of 140. The legal entries are necessary: P and S have
different owners’ claims and separate obligations. But the group has not yet sold that inventory
to anyone outside the group.
S sells two-fifths of the lot externally for 84, recording legal cost of sales 56, and pays P the full
internal invoice of 140. The diagram distinguishes the two sales. Only the second crosses the
group boundary.
After the external sale, S carries three-fifths of its purchase price, or 84. The same physical
stock cost the group only 3(60)/5 = 36. Its unrealised internal profit is therefore 84 −36 = 48.
IFRS 10.B86(c) requires full elimination of intragroup amounts and of profit still contained in
assets; it also directs the resulting temporary differences to IAS 12.
For clarity, let T be the internal price, C the group’s original cost and s the fraction sold
externally. The remaining internal profit is
U = (T −C)(1 −s) = (140 −60)(1 −2/5) = 48.
