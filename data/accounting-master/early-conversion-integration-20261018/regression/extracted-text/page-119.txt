9.4.2
A return right survives the sale
Consider 100 goods sold for 10 each, with a former inventory cost of 6 each. The buyer
obtains control, pays cash and has a contractual right to a refund for returned non-defective
goods. Assume an eligible Australian for-profit contract in 2024, within the retained AASB 15
compilation’s application period. The contract, control, price-estimation and significant-reversal
conditions are separately satisfied; immediate cash receipt alone would not establish them. The
numbers are synthetic measurements, not estimates of Apple’s returns.
If the company initially expects 10 goods to be returned, it expects to retain 900 of the 1,000
received. AASB 15 B20–B25 requires revenue for the expected entitlement, a refund liability
and a separate asset for the right to recover the goods (Australian Accounting Standards Board,
2022e). With no expected recovery costs or deterioration in this example, the refund liability
is 100 and the recovery asset is 60. The company debits cash 1,000, credits revenue 900 and
credits the refund liability 100. It removes inventory 600 by debiting cost of sales 540 and the
recovery asset 60. Pretax profit is therefore 360. Recognising the full 1,000 as revenue would
omit a known refund obligation, although the entry balances.
For a homogeneous cohort of N units, unit price p, former unit cost c and an admitted estimate
q of returns, the initial no-recovery-cost case gives
revenue = (N −q)p,
refund liability = qp,
cost of sales = (N −q)c,
recovery asset = qc.
The estimate and its measurement date are retained with the cohort. Paragraphs 53–59 require
an appropriate estimation method and the constraint on a significant reversal; q is not a
probability supplied by the accounting standard. Missing evidence for that measurement leaves
the amount unresolved. After actual returns begin, the state separately records refunded units,
remaining expected returns, remaining liability and recovery rights. A later outcome cannot be
inserted into the original information view.
Suppose the sale occurs in 2024 and new information at year end raises expected returns to
12. The liability increases by 20, reducing revenue by 20. The recovery asset increases by 12,
reducing cost of sales by 12. Closing revenue is 880, cost of sales 528 and pretax profit 352. The
change in expectations does not itself establish an error in the original estimate. If the opening
inventory of 600 was financed entirely by opening equity of 600, the resulting pretax balance
sheet has cash 1,000 and recovery asset 72, against refund liability 120 and equity 952.
In 2025, assume the return period ends with eight accepted returns and no remaining legal right
or unresolved claim. Before final settlement, remeasure the liability from 120 to 80 and the
recovery asset from 72 to 48. The first change credits revenue by 40; the second debits cost of
sales by 24. Refunding 80 then debits the liability and credits cash. Receiving the eight goods
debits inventory by 48 and credits the recovery asset by 48. Settlement does not recognise the
sale or its cost a second time. The 2025 pretax profit contribution is 16, closing cash is 920 and
inventory is 48. With no other movements, equity is 968: opening 600 plus cumulative profit
368.
The cash and accrual statements can now be checked independently. Operating cash is 1,000
in 2024 and −80 in 2025. In the indirect method, the first year adds the inventory reduction
and liability increase to profit while subtracting the recovery-asset increase. The second year
reverses the relevant balance movements:
CFO2024 = 352 + 600 −72 + 120 = 1,000,
CFO2025 = 16 −48 + 72 −120 = −80.
