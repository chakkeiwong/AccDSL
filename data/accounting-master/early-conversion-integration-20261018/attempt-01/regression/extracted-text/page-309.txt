Acquisition costs before the renewal contracts exist
An insurer may pay acquisition costs that help obtain both current contracts and their expected
renewals. Some of the related future groups do not yet exist as recognised insurance balances.
The retained AASB 17 provisions therefore require a separate acquisition-asset record for
each related group, unless the permitted immediate-expense election is used. Qualifying costs
are those directly attributable to the insurance portfolio; this is not a general permission to
capitalise selling expenses. A systematic and rational allocation distinguishes the original group
from groups expected to contain its renewals (Australian Accounting Standards Board, 2022f,
paragraphs 28A–28B, B35A and Appendix A).
Consider a separate, authored example in currency units. Follow only 60 of qualifying costs
already paid and allocated to expected renewals: 40 from one origination cohort to future
group G1, and 20 from another cohort to future group G2. Other acquisition allocations are
outside this example. Assume that both allocations are supported, neither future group has yet
been recognised, the immediate-expense election is not used, and facts now indicate possible
impairment. Those facts trigger the reporting-date recoverability assessment under paragraph
28E. An expected renewal remains a forecast, not a newly recognised premium receivable
(Australian Accounting Standards Board, 2022f, paragraphs 28B, 28E and 35).
Each future group contains the relevant renewals together with other new contracts. For G1,
suppose the whole group’s expected net inflow is 35, of which the renewal cohort contributes 25.
For G2, the corresponding amounts are 25 and 10. These are conditional measurements under
paragraph 32(a), incorporating the required cash-flow, time-value and risk adjustments. They
are neither gross premiums nor CSM. The acquisition cash already paid does not become a
second future outflow. In this additive example, the other contracts contribute 10 to G1 and 15
to G2.
Paragraph B35D(a) first tests the asset against its related group’s expected net inflow. G1’s
asset of 40 exceeds 35, so recognise impairment of 5 and retain 35. G2’s asset of 20 is below its
whole-group inflow of 25, so this test produces no loss for G2. Passing that first test does not
settle whether the renewals themselves can recover the costs allocated to them.
The second test addresses that narrower population. Under B35D(b), the allocated renewal
costs exceed the expected renewal inflows by 40 −25 = 15 for G1 and 20 −10 = 10 for
G2. But 5 of G1’s shortfall has already been recognised. B35D(b)(ii) requires that overlap
to be deducted, leaving additional losses of 10 for G1 and 10 for G2. Total impairment is
therefore 5 + 10 + 10 = 25, and the remaining assets are 25 and 10. Adding the first loss to the
two unadjusted renewal shortfalls would instead record 30, counting the same loss of 5 twice
(Australian Accounting Standards Board, 2022f, paragraph B35D).
For these disjoint allocations, write Ag for the original asset, Ng for the whole-group expected
net inflow and Ug for the relevant renewal inflow. With no prior impairment, the two losses and
the resulting balance are
Ig = max(Ag −Ng, 0),
Jg = max(Ag −Ug −Ig, 0),
Bg = Ag −Ig −Jg.
The subtraction of Ig inside the second test preserves the already recognised loss. This local
calculation relies on the identified, nonoverlapping renewal cohorts and positive inputs. A pool
with several overlapping origins would need an explicit allocation of the relevant costs, inflows
and prior losses before the same reasoning could be applied.
