Pillar Two is not another deferred-tax rate
The original September 2023 compilation predates the specific amendment adopted by Com-
mission Regulation (EU) 2023/2468 (European Commission, 2023b, arts. 2–3; annex 4A,88A–
88D,98M) of 8 November 2023, published 9 November 2023. Its Article 3 enters into force
the day after publication. Article 2 and the annex state the application and retrospective
rules. The retained amendment adds IAS 12.4A, 88A–88D and 98M. For legislation within
paragraph 4A, an entity neither recognizes nor discloses information about related deferred-tax
assets/liabilities, and discloses that it applied this exception. It separately discloses current
Pillar Two tax expense/income. Enacted or substantively enacted legislation not yet effective
requires known or reasonably estimable exposure information; unavailable information requires
the specified explanation and assessment progress.
The record therefore separates ordinary current/deferred tax from Pillar Two current tax,
exception status, applicability authority and exposure disclosures. A supplied in-scope current
top-up tax 7 creates current expense/payable 7 and later payment 7. A hypothetical Pillar Two
temporary difference 40 does not create a DTL 6 by multiplying by 15%. That is the wrong
target under the exception. Ordinary IAS 12 temporary differences continue under their own
rules. The current top-up amount 7 is an illustrative input, not a calculation of an actual GloBE
liability. Jurisdictional enactment, group thresholds, covered taxes, allocation, safe harbours
and current-law measurement remain separate required facts/methods before actual application.
The inspected amendment supplies the accounting boundary; it does not itself calculate tax
due for a company.
9.4.19
A shorter useful life can change two different histories
A carrying amount does not reveal whether the estimate behind it was sound. Suppose an
administrative asset costs 120, becomes available for use on 1 January 2023 and has no residual
value. The company records straight-line depreciation of 10 in each of 2023 and 2024, leaving
PPE of 100. On 1 April 2025, management concludes that the asset will be retired on 31
December 2028. That conclusion alone cannot tell us whether to change future expense or
correct the earlier accounts. We need the information that supported the old estimate, including
what was available when those accounts were authorized.
Consider two explicitly synthetic histories. In the first, twelve years was a reasonable useful life
at acquisition and at both year-end reviews. A newly enacted operating restriction on 1 April
2025 now limits use to the end of 2028. That restriction neither existed nor was anticipated at
the earlier dates. In the second history, the reliable, approved schedule at acquisition already
specified six years, but a transcription error entered twelve. The original schedule was available
and could reasonably have been obtained and considered at each authorization. Six years
remains appropriate; the new discovery exposes the old mistake. These histories differ in facts,
not in an accounting election available on the same known evidence.
AASB 108.5 makes that availability condition part of the definition of a prior-period error.

paragraphs 36–38 apply estimate changes to the current and relevant future periods (Australian

review of useful life and a method reflecting consumption (Australian Accounting Standards
